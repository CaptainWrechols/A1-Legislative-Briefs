## The headline

Healthcare-cost bills in Nevada mostly die **early and quietly, then pass
unanimously when they pass**: of 221 enacted policy bills, 187 carried a
unanimous floor vote in at least one chamber, while 95 died in their first
committee — concentrated in the four health and commerce committees. The
exceptions define the politics. Bills that cap prices, curb collection
tactics, or constrain insurer decision tools stop late: all fifteen vetoes
in the set fall in 2023 and 2025, and 2025 produced two finish-line deaths
in the end-of-session amendment shuttle — the PBM regulation rewrite
(SB316, 18–2 and 42–0) and the provider-shortage grant program (SB434,
18–2 and 42–0), which came back three months later and passed in the 36th
Special Session as SB5.

## Statement reality cards (ten)

Full structured cards live in `reality-map.json → proposal_reality_cards`. Summary:

1. **Licensure compacts** — *tried and split.* Compacts pass unanimously for PTs (2019 SB186, 2025 AB248), EMS personnel (2023 AB158), audiologists/SLPs (2025 AB230); dental-hygienist endorsement passed 42–0/21–0 (2025 AB334). The Nurse Licensure Compact died in its first committee three times — 2021 AB142, 2023 AB108, and 2025's five-compact omnibus SB34. The physician compact (IMLC) already sits in Nevada statute from before this window; 2025 SB124 (foreign medical graduates) passed 41–0/21–0.
2. **Provider supply** — *finished in the special session.* 2025 SB434 (Cannizzaro) created the Statewide Health Care Access and Recruitment Grant Program, passed 18–2 and 42–0, and died when the Senate did not concur in the final Assembly amendment; the 36th Special Session enacted the same program as SB5 (15–6, 37–0, Chapter 12) under the new Nevada Health Authority. Health Service Corps funding passed 2019/2021; the 2023 refill died in committee; the workforce-needs assessment (2025 SB495) passed the Senate 13–8 and died in the Assembly.
3. **GME / residencies** — *precedent exists.* The state residency grant program is law (2023 SB350, 21–0/40–0; revised 2025 SB262, 21–0/42–0, Sen. Pazina). The tax-credit funding design (Sen. Titus, R) died on the Senate floor twice (2023 SB369, 2025 SB269); the 2019 direct appropriation (AB311) died in committee.
4. **Provider loan forgiveness** — *already in statute and expanding.* Citizens thought this hadn't been drafted; it is the record's most reliable recruitment tool: Health Service Corps funded 2019 (SB289) and 2021 (SB233, Sen. Hardy R), underserved-communities loan repayment created 2023 (AB45, 41–0/21–0) and expanded twice in 2025 (AB269 40–2/21–0; SB266 42–0/21–0). The bigger-money versions died at money stages (2019 AB358, 2021 AB372, 2023 AB248, AB69).
5. **Prior authorization** — *partially done in 2025, veto on the AI piece.* AB463 (deadlines and rules for insurers, Medicaid, CHIP) is law, 42–0/20–0. SB128 (no AI-only denials, Sen. Neal) passed 23–16 and 15–6 and was vetoed. AB290 — the bill participants named — cleared its first committee, was re-referred, and died at session's end; AB295, AB470, SB398 died in first committees. All three 2019 attempts died in committee; step-therapy limits passed unanimously in 2023 (SB167, SB194).
6. **PBMs / middlemen** — *tried repeatedly, stalls late.* Law: the 2019 pharmacist gag-clause ban (AB141), drug-cost/rebate study (SB276), Medicaid rebate pass-through (SB378), 340B protections (2023 AB434), and the single state-contracted Medicaid PBM (2025 SB389, Scheible D + Stone R, 41–0/18–2). Died late: SB352 (2023, Senate 21–0, died in Assembly) and SB316 (2025, 18–2 and 42–0, died awaiting concurrence). Licensing designs died in first committees (2021 SB392, 2025 SB149/SB209). No PBM bill has ever lost a floor vote in this record.
7. **Network accuracy / credentialing** — *barely tried; thinnest record of the ten.* One direct law: 2019 SB234 (standardized network-rejection letters and trend reporting, 21–0/39–0). 2023 SB494 funded centralized Medicaid credentialing systems. No bill sets credentialing deadlines or audits directory accuracy. Sen. Hardy's two adjacent bills died (SB290 in committee 2019; SB90 in the second house 2021 after 21–0).
8. **Small-business / group pooling** — *precedent exists.* Association-plan and MEWA rules (2019 SB481, unanimous), public drug-purchasing pools (2021 SB396), and the Public Option (2021 SB420, 12–9/26–15 — the record's rare party-divided enactment). The any-group pool participants sketched has never been filed. A 2019 public-coverage study resolution (SCR10) was adopted.
9. **Reimbursement rates** — *narrow raises pass, broad reform stalls.* Enacted, mostly unanimous: autism behavior-tech rates (2021 SB96), provider-assessment funding for higher Medicaid rates (2023 SB435), children's cancer clinic rates (2023 SB221), family-caregiver pay (2025 SB185), training-clinic rates (2025 SB353), epilepsy devices (2025 AB284), autism rate caps removed (2025 SB497), vision-plan contract rules (2021 AB436, 2025 AB448). Died at the money stage: the cost-based rate study (2019 AB116), the rate-review overhaul (2023 AB99), children's rate raises twice (2023 SB255, 2025 SB239 — both Buck R), 2025's SB150 and SB366. Backdrop: the 31st (2020) Special Session's AB3 cut DHHS appropriations (36–6, 21–0).
10. **ER vs. urgent care / staffed clinics** — *the specific model has never been filed.* Existing edges: the 2019 out-of-network emergency billing law (AB469, 38–3/21–0), ER payment-dispute arbitration (2023 SB497), distinct identifiers for off-campus ER/urgent-care sites (2019 AB317), rural emergency hospitals (2023 AB277, 41–0/21–0), and settled telehealth law (2021 SB5, 2023 SB119, AB276). Facility price-control bills died early both times (2023 AB85, 2025 AB349 — both Orentlicher D).

## People and process signals (facts only)

- 270 of 409 policy bills came from named lawmakers; 139 from committees.
- Frequent primary sponsors: Sen. Pat Spearman (D, 21 — coverage and purchasing), Sen. Joseph Hardy (R, 19 — the Health Service Corps line and network-adjacent bills), Sen. Melanie Scheible (D, 18 — PBM bills including the enacted SB389), Sens. Cannizzaro (D, 16 — Public Option, shortage grants), Lange (D, 16), Stone (R, 16 — four PBM bills), Doñate (D, 16), Ohrenschall (D, 12).
- Cross-party sponsor teams: 54 policy bills; **36 became law** — and they cluster exactly on PBM, compact, and rate bills.
- Of the 95 first-committee deaths: Senate Health and Human Services 22, Assembly Commerce and Labor 21, Senate Commerce and Labor 19, Assembly Health and Human Services 16, money committees 10.
- **The veto pattern is new and specific:** all 15 vetoes fall in 2023 (8) and 2025 (7), on patient-cost and insurer-conduct bills — drug price caps (AB250, AB259), medical-debt collection limits (AB204), billing-error duties (AB282), the AI prior-auth ban (SB128), the forced-arbitration ban (AB439, vetoed after 42–0 in the Assembly), nurse staffing (SB182), and others.

## Deliberation prompts

1. The nurse compact died in its first committee three times while four other professions' compacts passed unanimously. What is different about nursing's committee audience, and what would change it?
2. SB434 and SB316 both passed both houses in 2025 and died in the amendment shuttle; one was revived in the special session, the other was not. What decides which finish-line deaths get a second chance?
3. Loan repayment for underserved-area providers is already in statute and keeps expanding. Is the gap a new program, or the size of the appropriation?
4. Prior-auth deadlines are now law (AB463) while the AI-denial ban was vetoed (SB128). What does the veto pattern imply for a refile?
5. Every broad Medicaid rate reform died at the money stage while narrow raises passed unanimously. Is scope the price of passage — and does that path ever add up to the systemic raise participants want?
6. The record's fifteen vetoes all fall in 2023–2025 and cluster on patient-cost bills. Which of the ten proposals are exposed, and which historically escape?
7. Cross-party teams carried 54 policy bills and 36 became law. Which proposals could plausibly attract one, judging by who already carries adjacent bills?
8. Network accuracy and credentialing have almost no legislative record. Is the lever regulatory rather than statutory — and what does SB234's reporting data show?
9. The ER-versus-urgent-care model has never been filed. Would a facility-licensing design (like the rural emergency hospital law) or an insurance-billing design (like the 2019 surprise-billing law) fit it better?

matched on ballots and 100% on sponsors; special-session facts come from a
manual verification file; 80:SCR10 is an adopted resolution the milestone
