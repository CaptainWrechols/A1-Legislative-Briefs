## 3) People and process signals (descriptive only; no party labels — roster join not fetched)

- **One room stops nearly everything:** of 163 first-committee deaths in the
  policy set, 149 were in a Judiciary committee — House Judiciary (84) and
  Senate Judiciary (65). Ethics, campaign finance, term limits, redistricting,
  RCV, FOIA, and recall all route there. The exceptions route to House
  Education and Public Works (school-board ethics, civics — 7) and House Ways
  and Means (earmark transparency — 5).
- **Who carried the accountability agenda:** Rep. Cobb-Hunter led 12 policy
  bills (redistricting commissions 123:H3390/H3432, 124:H4201/H4202/H4229,
  125:H3243/H3245/H3069; AG public financing 124:H3522; utility-money ban
  125:H3242; middle-school civics 126:H3547). Sen. Fanning led 10 (redistricting
  123:S230/S249/S254, 124:S750; e-filing 123:S253, 124:S309, 125:S195;
  pay-to-play bans 123:S231, 124:S306, 125:S199). Sen. McLeod led 7 (term
  limits every session). Rep. Taylor led 7 (Article V applications, FOIA
  review, RCV bans). Sen. Hembree led 7 (campaign bank accounts, ethics fines,
  and the enacted 126:S70).
- **Leadership signatures appear on disclosure, not limits:** the dark-money
  disclosure family (123:S3 Leatherman; 126:S960 Bennett–Hembree–Rankin–
  Turner–Massey–Campsen) and contributor-information bill (126:S813 Bennett–
  Peeler–Jackson–Rankin–Grooms–Sabb) carry Senate leadership names from both
  parties' benches — and still died in Senate Judiciary without votes.
- **High-support non-enactments (all of them):** 126:H3570 House 102–0 +
  Senate 40–0 (conference death); 124:S499 Senate 37–7 (died House Judiciary);
  123:S35 Senate 29–7 (died House Education); 125:H3676 House 68–30 (Senate
  calendar); 126:H5683 House 74–37 (Senate 26–18 to shelve); 124:S133 Senate
  24–16 + House 67–41 (House never took up the conference report); 125:H4561
  House 53–45 (died Senate Judiciary). Possible timing/process unfinished
  business, not proof an idea is dead — but H5683's motion to continue and
  H3570's 23–86 non-concurrence are recorded resistance, not accidents.
- **The one recorded "no" pattern:** nothing in this set lost a straight
  passage vote on the floor. Deaths happen earlier (committee silence) or
  later (conference, calendar, motion to continue) — never by voting a bill
  down at passage.

## 3b) Proposal reality cards

### [P-term-limits] Term limits / rotation rules for elected officials

- **What citizens proposed:** term limits or rotation rules so incumbents
  cannot hold the same office indefinitely (very high frequency, all 4
  events; mixed consensus — experience loss vs. entrenchment).
- **What lawmakers have tried:** 30 state-level measures in four sessions,
  in three designs: constitutional amendments capping service directly
  (three Senate/six House terms: 124:S302, 125:S172, 126:S590; stricter
  two/four: 125:H3574, 126:H5360), constitutional amendments authorizing
  the General Assembly to set limits by law (123:H3023/S944, 126:H3744),
  and straight statutes (123:H3024, 126:H3745; 126:H4462 pairs a 12-year
  cap with a pay raise).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** every one died in House or Senate
  Judiciary with no hearing and no vote — 30 for 30 across eight years.
- **What did move — aimed at Congress:** 126:H3008, the Article V
  application for a congressional term limits convention, was adopted by
  both chambers in May 2025 (Senate 29–14). Its predecessors 123:H3166/S663
  and 124:H3663 died in committee; the broad Convention of States
  application including federal term limits nearly passed twice (124:S133,
  125:H3676) before the single-subject version succeeded.
- **Who has carried them:** Reps. B. Cox/B.J. Cox filed the H3023/H3024
  pair in four straight sessions; Sen. McLeod filed Senate versions in
  three; Sens. Climer, Cash, Reichenbach on direct caps.
- **Levers and venue:** a state constitutional amendment needs a two-thirds
  vote of each chamber before voters ever see it — the members whose
  service would be capped. A statute-only cap (the H3024 design) may
  conflict with the constitution's qualifications clause, which is why the
  paired amendment + statute design keeps being filed. Rotation rules for
  committee chairs exist as a House-rule proposal (125:H4692) — an internal
  rule, not a law.
- **Open questions for a group:** does the two-thirds hurdle change which
  design (direct cap vs. authorize-by-law vs. chamber rule) is realistic?
  What would make Judiciary schedule the first hearing in eight years? Does
  the adopted federal application (H3008) signal anything about appetite
  for state-level limits, or does it show the difference between voting on
  Congress's terms and voting on one's own?

### [P-campaign-finance-reform] Limits on money in politics / campaign finance reform

- **What citizens proposed:** limit the influence of money in politics,
  including limits on PACs (very high frequency, high consensus;
  constitutional limits on regulating political spending noted).
- **What lawmakers have tried:** mostly disclosure, not limits — dark-money
  ad disclosure (123:S3, 124:S174, 126:S960), independent-expenditure
  reports over $500 (123:H4192), contributor occupation/employer disclosure
  (126:S813), filing actual bank statements with campaign reports (five
  bills, 123:S210 → 126:S91). Bans filed: corporate contributions
  (124:H4877), contributions from state-granted monopolies (125:H3242),
  pay-to-play appointments (four bills). One public-financing proposal, for
  Attorney General races only (124:H3522). The five contribution-limit
  bills filed all *raise* limits (tying them to federal indexed limits or
  higher caps), none lower them.
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** Senate or House Judiciary, no hearing,
  no vote — with one exception: 125:H4561 (letting candidates spend
  campaign funds on dependent care) passed the House 53–45, the record's
  only close floor vote, and died in Senate Judiciary.
- **Who has carried adjacent bills:** Senate leadership names from both
  benches on the disclosure bills (Leatherman, Bennett, Hembree, Rankin,
  Massey, Campsen, Peeler, Jackson, Sabb); Sen. Fanning and Sen. Young on
  the recurring families; Rep. Wetmore on H4561.
- **Levers and venue:** contribution *limits* and disclosure are state
  law (Title 8, Chapter 13); independent-expenditure *spending* caps face
  the federal constitutional constraint citizens themselves flagged
  (Citizens United) — which is why the filed bills chase disclosure
  instead. One Article V application on campaign finance (123:S192) died in
  committee.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the group's aim contribution limits,
  PAC/independent-expenditure disclosure, or public financing? Those have
  different legal ceilings and different bill families. Why would
  leadership-sponsored disclosure bills die in the leaders' own chamber's
  committee — drafting, priority, or something the record can't show?

### [P-ranked-choice-voting] Ranked choice voting

- **What citizens proposed:** ranked choice (instant runoff) voting (very
  high frequency; partial consensus — voter-confusion concerns raised).
- **What lawmakers have tried:** three enabling bills, all local-option —
  municipalities could adopt RCV (124:H5135, filed by Rep. B. Cox),
  instant-runoff for municipal elections (125:H4022, 126:H3589, Rep. J.L.
  Johnson) — and two statewide bans (125:H4591, 126:H3386, Rep. Taylor).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** all five died in House Judiciary with no
  hearing and no vote — the ban bills stopped in the same room as the
  enabling bills.
- **Nearest live wire:** South Carolina already runs statewide *runoff*
  primaries; the abolish-runoff bills (125:H3606, 126:H3552 — plurality
  wins) and instant-runoff bills solve the same runoff-cost problem in
  opposite ways, and neither direction moved.
- **Levers and venue:** election method is state law even for city
  elections (Titles 5 and 7), so a city cannot adopt RCV on its own — the
  local-option bills exist precisely to grant that permission. Military
  and overseas ballots already use an instant-runoff-style ranked ballot in
  runoff states, a fact groups may want to verify with the Election
  Commission when weighing feasibility.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the target statewide RCV, local
  option, or runoff replacement? Does the presence of ban bills change the
  strategy conversation? What does the Election Commission say
  implementation would cost, since no fiscal note was ever produced
  (no bill got far enough)?

### [P-independent-redistricting] Independent/nonpartisan redistricting commission

- **What citizens proposed:** move redistricting from the legislature to an
  independent or nonpartisan commission (high frequency, high consensus
  where discussed; the appointment question was the debated tradeoff).
- **What lawmakers have tried:** 21 bills across 2019–2024 in three
  designs — an independent commission written into the constitution
  (123:H3044/S6/S135, 124:H3279/S561, 125:H3173), a citizens commission
  created by statute that submits plans to the General Assembly
  (123:H3432/S254, 124:H4201/H4202, 125:H3243/H3245), and binding criteria
  without a commission (FAIR Act 124:H4229/S750, 125:H3069;
  Anti-Gerrymandering Act 125:H4222).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** House or Senate Judiciary, every time,
  no hearing, no vote. No commission bill was filed in the 126th session —
  the first session without one in this record.
- **What the legislature did instead:** drew the maps itself. Act 117 of
  2021 (H4493, House maps, 96–14/100–15/43–1) and Act 118 of 2022 (S865,
  Senate + congressional maps, 41–2 and 74–35/68–36/72–33) — the map later
  defended in the federal suit over District 1. In May 2026, a mid-decade
  congressional redraw (H5683) passed the House 74–37 after roughly fifty
  amendments were tabled, and the Senate shelved it 26–18 on a motion to
  continue; a companion (H4717) died in committee.
- **Who has carried them:** Rep. Cobb-Hunter (7 commission bills over three
  sessions), Sen. Fanning (4), Sens. Setzler/Davis and Rep. Clary on the
  earlier commission designs.
- **Levers and venue:** the constitution puts reapportionment in the
  General Assembly's hands, so a full independent commission needs a
  constitutional amendment — two-thirds of each chamber plus a statewide
  referendum. The statute-only designs (advisory citizens commissions,
  binding criteria) exist because of that hurdle.
- **Open questions for a group:** which design is the group actually
  proposing — advisory commission, binding criteria, or constitutional
  commission? Who appoints, the tradeoff citizens themselves flagged? Does
  the 2026 mid-decade fight (and the Senate's 26–18 shelving of it) change
  the opening for a criteria-based bill?

### [P-financial-disclosure] Better financial-disclosure and conflict-of-interest tracking (incl. PACs)

- **What citizens proposed:** strengthen financial disclosure and
  conflict-of-interest tracking for officials, including PAC ties (high
  frequency, high consensus; enforcement capacity the practical question).
- **What lawmakers have tried — and nearly done:** 126:H3570 rewrote who
  must file statements of economic interests and what they must show. It
  passed the House 102–0 (February 2025), passed the Senate 40–0 amended
  (May 2026), the House refused to concur 23–86, and it died in conference
  when the session ended — the closest any accountability bill came in this
  record. One slice became FY 2026-27 budget proviso 117.219 (officials
  reporting budget-paid income must name the paying agency).
- **What became law:** 126:S70, the School Board Ethics Act (Act 191 of
  2026, 39–2 and 109–4) — a required code of ethics and training for
  school board members, enacted after the 2019 versions (123:H3579,
  123:H4756) died in committee.
- **Where the rest stopped:** first committee, no votes: tax-return
  disclosure three times (123:S339, 124:S299, 125:S169), the e-filing
  audit department four times (123:H3435/S253, 124:S309, 125:S195),
  special-purpose-district coverage (124:S548, 125:S395), ethics-fine
  ballot bars (123:H3321, 124:S188, 125:S986, 126:S75), party-chairmen
  coverage (126:S1130).
- **Who has carried them:** Sen. Hembree (bank statements, fines, S70),
  Sen. Climer (tax returns, SPD coverage), Sen. Fanning (e-filing),
  Rep. Bannister and House leadership on H3570.
- **Levers and venue:** all state law (the Ethics Act, Title 8 Chapter 13);
  the State Ethics Commission's enforcement budget and even its reporting
  website are controlled through one-year budget provisos (110.1 gives the
  legislative ethics committees approval power over the disclosure
  website; 110.2 forces monthly meetings).
- **Open questions for a group:** what exactly divided the chambers in
  the H3570 conference — scope of who files, or what gets disclosed?
  (The record shows the 23–86 non-concurrence but not the reasoning.)
  Does enforcement capacity — the Ethics Commission's funding — belong in
  the proposal, given the proviso-level control the record shows?

### [P-voter-civics-education] Better voter education / civics and neutral civic information tools

- **What citizens proposed:** improve voter education and civics
  instruction, and provide neutral civic information tools (medium
  frequency, high consensus; who curates "neutral" was the open question).
- **What lawmakers have tried:** the REACH Act — college-level instruction
  on the founding documents — became law on its second try (124:S38,
  Act 26 of 2021, 45–0 and 91–12) after the 2019 version passed the Senate
  29–7 and died in House Education (123:S35). The K-12 design closest to
  the proposal — a required middle-school civics unit with student-led
  projects (126:H3547, Rep. Cobb-Hunter) — sits in House Education and
  Public Works without a vote. One content-restriction bill sits nearby
  (124:H4392, limits on civics course topics and funding).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** House Education and Public Works — the
  one theme whose chokepoint is not Judiciary.
- **What moved through the budget instead:** a Clemson feasibility study
  for a Center for Civic Engagement (FY 2024-25 proviso 45.11) and
  $2.5 million for USC's Center for American Civic Leadership and Public
  Discourse (FY 2026-27, inside proviso 118.21).
- **Levers and venue:** curriculum requirements are state law plus State
  Board of Education standards; voter-information tools would sit with the
  Election Commission (scvotes.gov) — no bill in this record proposes one.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the proposal curriculum (a REACH-style
  mandate for K-12), funding (the budget route already moving), or a voter
  information tool (never filed)? Who curates "neutral" — the question
  citizens themselves left open — and does the existence of
  content-restriction bills (H4392) change the design?

## 5) Deliberation prompts (questions, not answers)

1. If 92% of accountability bills die in the same two Judiciary committees
   without a hearing, what would earn hearing number one — a different
   committee route, leadership sponsorship (which already failed on S813
   and S960), or something outside the legislature?
2. Term-limit amendments need two-thirds of the members whose careers they
   would cap. Is the authorize-by-law design (H3744) a real path or the
   same hurdle in a different shape? What does the adopted congressional
   application (H3008) prove, if anything, about state-level appetite?
3. H3570 passed both chambers unanimously in different versions and died
   reconciling them. Is that a "no" on disclosure, or a solvable drafting
   fight? What was actually in the Senate amendment the House rejected 23–86?
4. When one piece of a dead bill (H3570) reappears as a one-year budget
   proviso (117.219), is that a win, a placeholder, or a way to avoid
   permanent law? Who decides whether it returns next year?
5. Both RCV-enabling and RCV-ban bills die unheard in the same committee.
   Does that mean the topic is radioactive, or just unprioritized?
6. The legislature shelved its own mid-decade map redraw 26–18 in the
   Senate. Does that vote tell a redistricting-commission group anything
   about which chamber to start in?
7. The only enacted ethics law in this set regulates school boards, and
   the only enacted civics law regulates colleges. What makes it easier to
   pass accountability rules for *other* bodies than for the General
   Assembly itself, and how should a proposal account for that?
8. Campaign-finance bills sponsored by Senate leadership died in Senate
   Judiciary. What would a group need to learn about why, before assuming
   a new version fares differently?
9. No bill in eight years proposes a neutral voter-information tool. Is
   that lane empty because nobody asked, or because it belongs to an
   agency rather than a statute?
