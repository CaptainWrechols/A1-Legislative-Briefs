Inference: unlike the accountability issue (where Judiciary is the wall),
this issue's bills die in the **money committees**: 138 of 161 policy bills
(86%) died in their first committee, and 58 of those 138 (42%) stopped in
House Ways and Means (41) or Senate Finance (17); House Education and Public
Works — the House's transportation committee — accounts for another 21.
Nothing in the set ever lost a passage vote on the floor; deaths happen by
committee silence. What passed travels one of two roads: broad-consensus
infrastructure housekeeping (utility relocation, fee collection, EV rules),
or a leadership-carried structural bill (SCDOT Modernization, Green Space
penny) — and the biggest money moved through the budget, not standalone law.

## 3) People and process signals (descriptive only; no party labels — roster join not fetched)

- **The wall is the money committees, not Judiciary:** of 138 first-committee
  deaths in the policy set, 41 were in House Ways and Means and 17 in Senate
  Finance (58 combined, 42%) — because most growth-and-roads ideas are tax
  or fee ideas. House Education and Public Works (the House transportation
  committee) stopped 21; the Judiciary committees 25; the House
  Medical/Military/Municipal committee (local government bills) 10; Senate
  Transportation 8.
- **Nothing lost a floor vote:** no bill in this set ever failed a passage or
  reading vote. Every death is committee silence, calendar death, or (once)
  a recommittal after floor debate stalled (126:H5071).
- **Who carried the agenda:** Rep. Pendarvis led 12 policy bills — nearly the
  whole transit/multimodal lane (123:H3654/H3655/H3656/H3828, 124:H3051,
  125:H4013/H5347) plus penny stacking (124:H3129), EV-fee repeals
  (124:H4945, 125:H3177), and a gas-fee suspension (124:H5112). Sen. Davis
  led 7 — the enacted Green Space penny (124:S152) and its follow-ons, the
  penny expansions (125:S562), workforce-housing penny bills, and the 2026
  concurrency bill (126:S227). Rep. Hixon led 7 (the Rural Infrastructure
  Authority package and transferred-roads maintenance 125:H4610). Sen. Scott
  led 6 (transit funding, broadband, transportation authorities). Sen. Grooms
  led 4 including the enacted SCDOT Modernization Act (126:S831). Rep. White
  led 4 — the 2026 SCDOT accountability package filed together in March 2026
  (road-hazard reports 126:H4687, gas-fee county share 126:H5331, dissolve
  the commission 126:H5362, pavement preservation 126:H5363), all dead in
  committee while S831 passed. Sen. Hutto led the three I-95 toll bills.
- **High-support non-enactments (policy set):** 125:H3075 (House 112–0, died
  Senate Finance), 124:H4817 (House 106–3, died Senate Finance), 125:H3737
  (House 65–46, died Senate Finance) — all three House passages of this
  record died in the same room. Single-instance patterns; labeled history,
  not destiny.
- **The 126th pattern:** the SCDOT governance fight ended with the Senate
  bill (S831) passing 114–0 in the House while the House's own H5071 was
  recommitted — one chamber's version wins, the idea passes anyway.

## 3b) Proposal reality cards

Five cards, per the FINAL proposal grid ("SC1 - Growth Infrastructure
Roads", received 2026-09-04), which supersedes the seven-proposal draft
grid this issue's config originally carried: the standalone developer-pays
proposal folded into local-funding-tools (whose title names impact fees),
and the multimodal-transport proposal was dropped (its bills remain in the
theme scorecards above and Appendices A-C).

### [P-local-funding-tools] New or expanded local funding tools (penny / sales tax, impact fees, tolls, parking fees)

- **What citizens proposed (final grid):** give local governments new or
  expanded funding tools — penny/sales taxes, impact fees, tolls, parking
  fees. High frequency (Columbia, Florence, Charleston); high consensus —
  broad agreement the state needs to look for new sources of funding;
  tradeoff: people don't like being taxed if they don't know where the
  money is going, so any taxes should include greater transparency.
- **What already exists:** counties can already levy a transportation penny
  tax (with tolls) by referendum (Title 4 Ch. 37) and a capital project
  penny (Title 4 Ch. 10) — the record is about widening those tools.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** transit-eligibility clarifications for the
  transportation penny in all four sessions (123:S611/H4389, 124:H3535/S437,
  125:S290/S562/H4059, 126:S979), penny stacking (124:H3129), 11-year
  reimposition (125:S116), grocery carve-outs (125:H5188/S1113), five I-95
  toll bills (123:S178/H3739/S780, 125:S499/S674), a local-option gas tax
  (123:S172), and a road-use-fee transparency account (124:H3483).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** Senate Finance, House Ways and Means, or
  Senate Transportation — first committee, no hearing, no vote, all of them.
- **What did pass:** the County Green Space Sales Tax Act (124:S152, Act 166
  of 2022, Senate 43–1/41–3, House 67–28) — the one genuinely new local
  penny, for land preservation, voter-approved by referendum; plus
  flexibility acts: tourism taxes for flooding/drainage (123:S217, Act 146),
  beach-parking rules with revenue directions (124:S40, Act 89), and the
  school-buildings penny opened to more counties (126:H4589, Act 203,
  House 81–18/91–14, Senate 40–2).
- **Who has carried them:** Sen. Davis (S152 and the penny-expansion
  family), Sen. Scott (transportation authorities and transit funding),
  Sen. Hutto (the I-95 tolls), Rep. Pendarvis (penny stacking).
- **Levers and venue:** every local tax here requires a county referendum —
  the transparency condition citizens attached is structurally present in
  the ballot-question requirements (projects must be listed). Tolls on
  interstates also need federal approval, which no filed bill reached.
- **The impact-fee tool the proposal names:** legal since 1999 (the
  Development Impact Fee Act, 6-1-910 et seq., with strict conditions — a
  capital improvements plan first, and no funding of maintenance or
  operations). Ten bills in three sessions pulled in both directions —
  expanding it (residential-only fees 125:H4659, resurfacing as a fundable
  cost 126:H5088, a gentrification-fund developer fee 124:H3460/126:H4008,
  pre-1999 debt payoff 124:H4943) and narrowing it for the building
  industry (cost exclusions 125:H4981/S856/H5017, 126:H3165; repeat-buyer
  exemption 126:H4672). Zero hearings in either direction, and the one
  enacted impact-fee rule blocked the fees: FY 2021-22 proviso 117.96
  (school construction, one year, not renewed). Carriers: Rep. Bailey and
  coastal members on the narrowing side; Rep. Pendarvis on the
  gentrification fee.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the aim a new tool, or making the
  existing penny and impact-fee tools usable for the costs citizens named?
  Why does the preservation penny pass while transportation-penny
  expansions and impact-fee bills never get a hearing in the same
  committees? How does the group answer the school-proviso precedent — the
  one legislative action on impact fees blocked them?

### [P-state-master-planning] State comprehensive/regional planning instead of fragmented county plans

- **What citizens proposed (final grid):** state comprehensive/master
  planning vs. fragmented county plans; regional control. Very high
  frequency (all 4 conversations); mixed consensus — some cited efficiency
  gains from centralized planning while others remained skeptical of
  removing local control; a consensus recommendation in Charleston;
  tradeoff: removing local control could let the state go against
  residents' wishes.
- **The blunt finding:** no bill in this record proposes state or regional
  master planning. The lever citizens described has not been filed.
- **What exists instead — state rules on local plans:** the Disaster Relief
  and Resilience Act (123:S259, Act 163 of 2020, Senate 44–1, House 65–35)
  created the state Office of Resilience, a statewide resilience plan, and
  a required resiliency element in every local comprehensive plan — the
  record's one enacted statewide planning layer (its House twin 123:H4731
  died in Judiciary).
- **The nearest live design — concurrency:** letting local governments
  require that infrastructure keep pace before development is approved
  (125:H5562 died in committee; 126:S227 was reported favorably, amended
  on the Senate floor in the 2026 session's final weeks, and died without
  completing passage; its House twin 126:H4050 never moved). A
  community-impact-assessment requirement for zoning decisions (126:H4390)
  also died in committee.
- **The annexation front:** county standing to challenge annexations
  (125:H4651), county power to block annexation until an infrastructure
  impact study is approved (126:H5742), and the thrice-filed donut-hole
  annexation bill (124:H5196, 125:H3236, 126:H4726) all died in committee.
- **The counter-current:** the developer-side bills loosen local control —
  Home Attainability permitting packages (124:S528/H3863, 125:S4, 126:S4),
  45-day permit shot clocks (125:H4652, 126:H3215), TDR enabling
  (125:H4996, 126:H4146/S288). Also all dead, but pulling the opposite
  direction. The enacted Small Wireless Facilities Act (123:H4262,
  Act 179) preempted local zoning for 5G cells — the one enacted
  state-over-local land-use rule in the record.
- **Levers and venue:** comprehensive planning is Title 6 Ch. 29 (local,
  with nine required elements); a state or regional planning body would be
  new law. The resilience act shows the add-an-element path works; the
  concurrency bills show the growth-management path stalls at the last
  step.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the proposal state planning, regional
  coordination, or state *rules* for local plans (the only path with an
  enactment)? How does the group resolve the home-rule tension citizens
  themselves flagged — the record shows bills pulling both directions
  dying in the same committees?

### [P-fix-roads-first] Maintain existing roads before building new capacity

- **What citizens proposed (final grid):** fix the roads we've got first.
  Medium frequency (Columbia and the online session); mixed consensus —
  broad agreement that maintenance needs to be factored in, but many
  participants want to see expanded infrastructure; tradeoff: expansion
  expense versus maintaining what exists, with worry that too much goes to
  capital projects and not enough to maintenance.
- **What lawmakers have tried (bills):** the Fix Our Roads Accountability
  Act — a statewide pavement preservation program with annual reports
  (126:H5363); SCDOT must maintain roads transferred into the state system
  (125:H4610); pothole-damage hotlines (124:H3871, 125:H3451); sheriff
  road-hazard reporting with a public database (126:H4687); county
  maintenance of orphan private roads (125:H3516). Every one died in first
  committee.
- **Where the idea is real — the budget:** proviso 86.1 (renewed every
  enacted year) requires the Act 40 increase to County Transportation
  Committees be spent exclusively on repairs, maintenance, and
  improvements to the state highway system. The one-time lists:
  FY 2024-25 put $200,000,000 into a CTC Acceleration Fund, $100,000,000
  into a Bridge Acceleration Fund, and $117,401,000 into a Rural Road
  Safety Program; FY 2025-26 added $200,000,000 for Bridge Modernization;
  FY 2026-27 added $175,000,000 (CTC) and $50,000,000 (bridges) and
  created the Road Buyback Program (proviso 84.18) — the state paying
  counties to take back state roads, resurfaced first. SCDOT's
  maintenance-versus-expansion split itself is set in its own commission
  plan, not in statute.
- **Who has carried the bills:** Rep. White's four-bill 2026 package
  (H4687, H5331, H5362, H5363) is the most direct fix-first agenda filed;
  Rep. J.L. Johnson the hotline bills; Rep. Hixon the transferred-roads
  rule.
- **Levers and venue:** the maintenance money is annual-budget territory
  (where it has moved); a statutory preservation program (H5363's design)
  would make it permanent law. Roads belong 63%-ish to the state system —
  the buyback program and H4971's devolution design would shift that split.
- **Open questions for a group:** should fix-first live in permanent law
  (the unheard H5363) or keep riding the budget (where it actually moves)?
  Is the Road Buyback Program a fix-first tool or a cost-shift to counties?

### [P-no-new-taxes] Oppose new taxes or fees for infrastructure

- **What citizens proposed (final grid):** no new taxes / oppose extra
  taxes and fees. Medium frequency (multiple tables at Columbia and
  Florence); low consensus — sharp disagreement over whether new taxes are
  essential for improvements or the state should prioritize keeping costs
  low; some participants argued the state's low taxes are one reason
  infrastructure suffers.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** ten measures against the gas user fee —
  freeze the Act 40 phase-in at eight cents (124:H4091), repeal it
  (124:H4092), suspend it for a year or until prices fall (124:H5103,
  124:H5112 in the 2022 spike), and the 2026 wave (126:S1045 plus five
  near-identical House resolutions 126:H5398/H5419/H5422/H5443/H5475).
  Also: repeal the EV road-use fee (124:H4945, 125:H3177).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** House Ways and Means or Senate Finance,
  every one, no hearings — the same committees that stop the new-tool
  bills. Neither side of the tax split has gotten a vote.
- **The other direction:** 126:H5331 would more than double the county
  share of the gas fee; it died in the same committee. And collection was
  tightened, not loosened: the titling fix (124:H3505, Act 70, 106–4/42–2)
  closed a gap in the road-funding vehicle fee.
- **Who has carried them:** Rep. Haddon (the freeze/repeal pair plus the
  SCDOT expenditure report), Rep. Pendarvis and others on suspensions —
  the suspension resolutions have sponsors from multiple directions.
- **Levers and venue:** the gas fee is state law (12-28); suspensions were
  drafted as joint resolutions. Every *local* tool in the record is
  referendum-gated, which is where this proposal and local-funding-tools
  actually meet: voters decide.
- **Open questions for a group:** given the split citizens themselves
  showed, is the shared ground the referendum requirement? What would a
  no-new-taxes position say about the budget's one-time transfers
  ($400M+ in FY 2024-25) — are those "no new taxes" in practice?

### [P-contractor-accountability] Improve the bidding process and hold contractors accountable

- **What citizens proposed (final grid):** improve the contractor bidding
  process / hold contractors accountable. Medium frequency (the online
  conversation and Greenville); high consensus — participants widely agreed
  this could be a low-cost fix; participants felt the state was not getting
  quality contractors for road repairs and pushed for more standards and
  transparency in bidding.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** SCDOT design-build contracting pilots
  (125:H5312, 126:H3560), the 2020 joint resolutions approving SCDOT's
  contractor performance-evaluation and contractor-disqualification
  regulations (123:S1069, 123:S1070 — placed on the Senate calendar and
  stranded when COVID ended the session), SCDOT expenditure-report
  certification (124:H4090), DBE contracting goals (123:S385, H4401,
  H4823), and the public-private partnership framework (124:H3559). None
  received a floor vote; the regulation resolutions are the only ones that
  even reached a calendar.
- **What was enacted at the edges:** utility-relocation cost assignment on
  road projects (123:S401, Act 36 of 2019, 38–0/108–0; extended to 2032 by
  126:H3768, Act 244 of 2026, 103–0/44–0) and the contractor-licensing
  rewrite (125:H4115, Act 69 of 2023 — licensing, not road bidding).
- **Where the accountability tools actually live:** budget provisos. SCDOT
  must publish its project priority lists with the ranking methodology
  (84.8/84.9, renewed yearly); FY 2024-25 onward directs the Programmed
  Project Viewer public dashboard upgrade — status, forecast versus actual
  cost, completion dates, an on-time/on-budget list, quarterly reports
  (84.18 → 84.16 → 84.15). SCDOT's contractor rules themselves are agency
  regulations, which the legislature reviews by joint resolution.
- **Who has carried them:** Rep. Brewer (design-build, the Act 36
  extension, railroad-crossing fees), Rep. Haddon (expenditure reports).
- **Levers and venue:** SCDOT procurement sits in Title 57 and agency
  regulation, not the general Procurement Code; the dashboard and
  priority-list rules are one-year provisos that would lapse if not
  renewed.
- **Open questions for a group:** is the aim bidding method (design-build
  died twice), performance consequences (the stranded 2020 regulations),
  or public visibility (the proviso dashboard — already moving)? Should
  one-year transparency provisos become permanent statute?

## 4) Recent enactments watchlist (126th, 2025–2026)

- 126:S831 — the SCDOT Modernization Act (Act 177 of 2026, Senate 37–1,
  House 114–0, conference 112–2/43–0): the Governor now appoints the
  Secretary of Transportation and the commission is abolished January 1,
  2027, its duties devolved. An omnibus, not governance alone: it also
  created the Pothole Mitigation Program (57-5-1800: public reporting,
  seven-day repairs, $15M/yr), enacted phased design-build and CM/GC
  contracting (57-5-1710/1720), rewrote toll rules around new-capacity
  "choice lanes", authorized P3s up to 60 years, required a four-year
  external audit, and reset the county "C"-funds split to 33% state-highway.
  The biggest structural answer to "who is accountable for roads" in
  decades; groups may ask what it changes for project selection.
- 126:H3768 — Act 244 of 2026 (103–0/44–0): extends the utility-relocation
  cost rule to 2032 and consents to federal NEPA-assignment suits.
- 126:H4589 — Act 203 of 2026 (81–18/40–2/91–14): the school-buildings

   this set without hearings. What would earn hearing number one for a
   penny-tax expansion or an impact-fee bill — and does the Green Space
   penny's path (leadership sponsor, referendum-gated, preservation
   framing) transfer?

   the same legislature that created a preservation penny never hear a

   impact-fee rule blocked the fees for schools. Is the 1999 Act's

   other unheard structural bills in this set?
