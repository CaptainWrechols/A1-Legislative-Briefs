## 3) People and process signals (descriptive only; no party labels — roster join not fetched)

- **Four committees hold this issue.** Utility bills route to House Labor,
  Commerce and Industry and Senate **Judiciary** (in South Carolina, utility
  law goes to Senate Judiciary, not a commerce committee); tax bills route to
  House Ways and Means and Senate Finance. Of 257 policy bills, 201 died
  in their first committee, and South Carolina publishes no committee vote
  counts — so for five of every six bills there is no recorded vote of any
  kind.
- **Senate Judiciary is where House-passed utility bills stop.** Four
  utility-accountability bills — H4260 (105–1), H4776 (101–0), H4149
  (118–0), H3614 (114–0) — died there without a floor vote. Combined House
  support: 438 yes, 1 no. The House-passed domestic-violence rental bill
  (H4158, 98–10) stopped in the same committee before its 2026 successor
  passed.
- **House Ways and Means is where homestead bills stop.** 45 of the 67
  home-property-tax bills died there, including Senate-passed S768 (44–0).
- **Who carried what** (lead sponsor, from the official sponsor line):
  Rep. King led 14 policy bills — the utility consumer-protection lane
  (payment fees, shutoffs) and full senior exemptions; every one died in
  first committee. Rep. Jones led 12, nearly all 2025-26 housing-supply and
  affordability bills; all died in first committee. Rep. Hart refiled the
  property-valuation constitutional amendment and millage-cap change every
  session. Sen. Young led the Natural Gas Rate Stabilization rewrites and
  utility-privacy bills each session. Rep. Ott led the whistleblower
  Ratepayer Protection Acts that passed the House 118–0 and 114–0.
  Rep. G.M. Smith (House Ways and Means chair) and House Speaker Lucas led
  the enacted packages (H3194 Santee Cooper; H5118/H3309 energy; H3770,
  H5075 housing credits). Sen. Peeler led S1087 (tax cut), S439, and S768.
  Rep. Burns led the elimination and big-exemption bills (H3378, H3380,
  H5264).
- **High-support non-enactments (top of list):** 124:H4149 House 118–0;
  125:H3614 House 114–0; 124:H4880 House 112–0; 124:H3921 House 111–0
  (context); 125:H5066 House 106–2; 123:H4260 House 105–1; 125:H5014 House
  102–0; 123:H4776 House 101–0; 126:S768 Senate 44–0; 124:S16 both chambers

## 3b) Proposal reality cards

### [P-utility-transparency] Greater transparency into utility rates and legislator/utility financial ties

- **What citizens proposed:** open up how utility rates are set, and disclose
  financial ties between legislators and utilities.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** a lot, with a sharp split. The exact
  legislator-utility-money concern appears in the record: the 2019 Ratepayer
  Protection Act (123:H4260) would have banned utilities from giving campaign
  contributions, meals, lodging, or anything of value to members of the
  Public Utilities Review Committee — the legislators' panel that screens
  utility regulators — and banned utility-tied people from serving as
  regulators. It passed the House 105–1 and died in Senate Judiciary. Its
  refiles narrowed to whistleblower protections and kept passing the House
  (118–0 in 2022, 114–0 in 2023) and kept dying in the same committee.
  Revolving-door bans (one year → three or four years) followed the same
  path: H4776 passed the House 101–0 and died in Senate Judiciary.
- **What became law instead:** oversight machinery. Act 90 of 2021
  (124:H3194) rebuilt Santee Cooper's board and gave the PSC rate review
  over it for the first time; Acts 74/75 of 2025 (126:S271, 126:H4402)
  restarted PSC elections after commissioners sat on expired terms; the 2025
  Energy Security Act (126:H3309) added public-witness rights in rate cases
  and deadlines for testimony. The PSC's Santee Cooper oversight is funded
  through a budget proviso (72.3), not permanent law.
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** Senate Judiciary, every time, without a
  vote — four House-passed utility-accountability bills in eight years
  (combined House vote 438–1).
- **Who carried adjacent bills:** Rep. Caskey (H4260), Rep. Ott (H4149,
  H3614), Rep. Clary (2019 PSC-structure bills), Sen. Talley/Sen. Davis
  (2024 PSC restructuring), Rep. Reese (2026 citizens council and billing
  accountability).
- **Levers and venue:** rates themselves are set case-by-case by the Public
  Service Commission, not the legislature; the legislature's levers are who
  sits on the PSC, what procedures it must follow, and what utilities may
  spend money on. Legislators' personal finances are already disclosed
  annually on Statements of Economic Interests under the state ethics law
  (Title 8) — those forms are public but not utility-specific. No bill in
  this record adds a utility-specific disclosure duty for legislators; the
  filed approach regulated the utilities' side (no gifts or contributions to
  the screening panel).
- **What a group would still need to learn:** why Senate Judiciary has not
  taken up the House-passed bills (no committee record exists); whether the
  2025 public-witness and PSC-election changes address the concern or leave
  the money-ties piece open; what an SEI form does and does not show about
  utility ties.

### [P-utility-competition] Open the utility market / end the monopoly / lower competition barriers

- **What citizens proposed:** allow competition in the utility market by
  ending monopoly service territories or lowering barriers to entry.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** the state has officially studied it — once,
  seriously. Act 187 of 2020 (123:H4940) created the Electricity Market
  Reform Measures Study Committee, required an independent expert
  consultant, and passed the House 81–31 (the most contested vote of any
  enacted bill in this set) and the Senate 41–0. Every bill that would
  actually open the retail market was filed in the 2025–26 session and died
  in its first committee without a vote: retail choice for households
  (126:H5439), third-party suppliers (126:H5440, 126:S878), and PSC
  oversight of the co-ops' wholesale market (126:H5525).
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** first committee — House Labor, Commerce
  and Industry and Senate Judiciary. No recorded vote exists on any
  market-opening bill.
- **Adjacent record:** 124:S439 would have forced Santee Cooper to divest
  generation and join a regional transmission organization — first
  committee. 126:S12 (joint ownership of plants with private utilities)
  produced the record's closest utility floor vote, 25–18, before being
  recommitted.
- **Who carried adjacent bills:** Rep. Brewer (H5439, H5440), Rep. C.
  Mitchell (H5525), Sen. Davis (S998, the Senate study version).
- **Levers and venue:** service territories and retail regulation are state
  statute — this is squarely a General Assembly lever. Wholesale markets and
  transmission access are federal (FERC) territory, which is why several
  bills work through RTO-membership and wholesale-access angles.
- **What a group would still need to learn:** what the 2020 study committee
  concluded (its report is outside this bill record); the citizens' own
  flagged tradeoff — whether grid infrastructure supports competition; and
  why the 2026 bills arrived only after the 2024–25 rate debates.

### [P-financial-education] Personal budgeting and financial education

- **What citizens proposed:** teach personal budgeting and financial skills
  so households can manage rising costs.
- **What lawmakers have tried — and what already exists:** this proposal is
  the one that is already policy, though not through a bill. South Carolina
  has required financial-literacy instruction offerings since 2005 (Section
  59-29-410). Six bills from 2019–2022 tried to make personal finance a
  graduation requirement: 123:H3199 passed the House 102–10 and died in
  Senate Education; 124:S16 passed the Senate 44–1 and the House 109–0 in
  differing versions and died in conference in 2022. That same year the
  requirement went around the bill process: FY 2022-23 budget proviso 1.101
  ordered the State Board of Education to write a half-credit personal
  finance graduation requirement into regulation. Regulation Document 5130
  (amending R.43-234) took effect May 26, 2023; it binds beginning with the
  2023-24 freshman class, so the Class of 2027 is the first that must earn
  the credit. The approval joint resolution (125:S732) was recommitted and
  never passed — the regulation took effect under the APA's review clock
  anyway.
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** Senate Education (H3199), House Education
  and Public Works (four refiles), and conference (S16).
- **Who carried adjacent bills:** Sen. Rankin (S15, S16), Rep. Govan (H3199,
  H3022), Rep. Davis (H4149, H3116).
- **Levers and venue:** split. The graduation requirement lives in State
  Board of Education regulation; course content standards live with the
  education department; the statute (59-29-410) and any funding live with
  the General Assembly. Nothing in this record addresses adult financial
  education — every filed bill was about high school students.
- **What a group would still need to learn:** whether the proposal is
  already satisfied by the 2023 requirement (first affected class graduates
  in 2027 — no outcome data exists yet); whether the citizens' intent
  included adults, which no bill has touched; the citizens' own flagged
  tradeoff — education does not lower prices.

### [P-reduce-property-vehicle-taxes] Reduce or eliminate property and vehicle taxes

- **What citizens proposed:** reduce or eliminate property taxes and vehicle
  taxes to lower household costs.
- **What lawmakers have tried:** more bills than any other lane in this
  issue — 80 property/vehicle tax bills — with the narrowest results.
  Full elimination was filed once: 126:H3378 (exempt all property, with
  dollar-for-dollar state reimbursement) died in House Ways and Means
  without a vote. Roughly two dozen bills to raise the $50,000
  senior/disabled homestead exemption (to $75,000, $100,000, the full home
  value, or new age groups) died the same way — except 126:S768 ($100,000
  and age 60), which passed the Senate 44–0 and then died in House Ways and
  Means. Thirteen vehicle/boat tax bills produced zero recorded votes; the
  broadest (126:H5014, one car exempt for seniors) died in House Ways and
  Means. No bill proposes eliminating the vehicle property tax statewide.
- **What became law instead:** narrow, near-unanimous fixes — disabled
  veterans (Act 116 of 2024), surviving spouses and heirs' property (Act 236
  of 2022), estate continuity (Act 140 of 2026), installment payments (Act
  69 of 2021) — plus one structural option: the Municipal Tax Relief Act
  (126:S866, Act 228 of 2026, Senate 32–9, House 96–16) lets certain cities
  swap up to a 1% local sales tax for owner-occupied property tax credits,
  by referendum. And the biggest recent relief is one-year budget relief:
  FY 2026-27 proviso 117.220 adds an extra $25,000 homestead exemption on
  top of the standing $50,000, and provisos 117.208/117.191 put income tax
  rate cuts into effect faster than the statutes' triggers.
- **Where similar ideas stopped:** House Ways and Means (45 of 67 home
  property tax bills) and Senate Finance (11).
- **Who carried adjacent bills:** Rep. Burns (elimination and big
  exemptions), Rep. Long and Rep. Moss (homestead increases, refiled every
  session), Sen. Peeler (S768 and the enacted S439/S1087), Rep. Hart
  (valuation amendments), Rep. King (senior full exemptions and the
  car-tax-payment bills).
- **Levers and venue:** property tax is local revenue under state rules —
  schools and local governments are funded by it, which is the tradeoff the
  citizens themselves flagged. Existing law already exempts owner-occupied
  homes from school operating taxes (Act 388 of 2006's swap, reimbursed
  through the Homestead Exemption Fund, Section 11-11-156) — so "eliminate
  property taxes on homes" is partly done for one tax line and would require
  replacing local revenue for the rest. The vehicle property tax is likewise
  county revenue collected at registration.
- **What a group would still need to learn:** the price tag of each design
  (none of the failed bills carries a fiscal note in this record); how
  replacement revenue would work — the enacted S866 model uses a local sales
  tax swap, the citizens' concern; why House Ways and Means has not taken up
  even the Senate-passed S768 (no committee record exists).

## 3c) Legislator-discussion topic: child care assistance (added 2026-09-04; not a Phase 2 proposal)

Requested in connection with reported discussions among sitting legislators
about possible child care assistance. Full scan:
`childcare-assistance-scan.md` (this directory). Record shape, descriptive:

- **What passes is regulation and governance, never assistance.** Three acts:
  background checks for childcare staff (123:S595, Act 52 of 2019, 39–0 and
  100–1), First Steps board restructuring (125:H4023, Act 81 of 2023, 41–0
  and 107–0), caregiver training requirements (125:S862, Act 216 of 2024).
- **The near-miss:** 125:S946 (childcare regulations modernization) passed
  the Senate 45–0 and the House 105–0 in differing versions and died in
  conference at the 2024 session's end — the same conference-timing stop
  that preceded other later enactments in this record.
- **Every assistance/affordability design died in a money committee without
  a vote:** workforce childcare stipends for unemployed parents (126:H4394,
  House Ways and Means); a bigger employer childcare credit plus a new
  childcare-worker credit (126:S47 / 126:H4015, Senate Finance / House Ways
  and Means); making the child and dependent care credit refundable
  (126:H5794 — filed August 2026, the newest bill in the whole set); the
  Childcare Advance Act deferral (125:H4993); pre-K expansion grants
  (125:H5205). The conditions design runs the other way: 126:S770 would add
  work requirements to federally funded assistance (first committee).
- **The base machinery already exists:** two childcare tax credits in
  statute (employer credit §12-6-3440; child & dependent care credit
  §12-6-3380 — the filed bills extend, not invent, this machinery); the
  assistance program itself is federal money run through DSS, regulated
  annually by proviso (DSS: Child Care Voucher, 38.18 FY 2021-22 then 38.17
  every cycle; DSS: Childcare Provider Fraud, 38.28, new FY 2026-27); state
  pre-K (Full-Day 4K / CERDEP) expands through provisos every cycle.
- **Signals distinguishing this from the zero-vote lanes** (descriptive,
  not advice): filings are accelerating (three childcare-assistance designs
  in the 126th, including one filed in August 2026); every design uses the
  tax code — the venue where this record's cost-relief measures do pass
  (S1087, H4216, S866); S946 shows childcare bills win unanimous floor votes
  when they reach the floor; and both directions (expansion and conditions)
  are filing, which reads as an active, unsettled lane rather than a dead
  one. What has never been filed: any direct-payment (non-tax-code) state
  subsidy to families or providers, and any state-funded expansion beyond
  the federal voucher money.
- **Certainty:** medium-high for the committee pattern (8 assistance bills,
  zero votes); the "acceleration" signal rests on one session and one
  August 2026 filing.

## 5) Deliberation prompts (questions, not answers)

1. Four utility-accountability bills passed the House with a combined 438–1
   vote and died in Senate Judiciary without a hearing record. What would
   have to change for the fifth to be different?
2. The 2020 market-reform study committee was created by law. What did it
   conclude, and does its report answer the feasibility worry raised in the
   Community Conversations?
3. Retail-choice bills appeared for the first time in 2026, right after two
   years of rate debates. Is that momentum, or a one-session reaction?
4. The personal-finance graduation requirement is in force, but the first
   affected class graduates in 2027. What would "this proposal is done"
   versus "this proposal needs more" look like?
5. Homestead-increase bills die in House Ways and Means even after a 44–0
   Senate vote — yet the same chamber wrote a one-year $25,000 exemption
   into the budget. Is the objection to the policy, or to making it
   permanent?
6. Full property-tax elimination (H3378) promises dollar-for-dollar state
   reimbursement. What state revenue would replace roughly what share of
   local budgets, and does the S866 sales-tax-swap model scale?
7. No bill in eight years proposes ending the vehicle property tax
   statewide. Is that because nobody has tried, or because county budgets
   depend on it?
8. Big energy packages pass; consumer billing protections never get a vote.
   Are the billing ideas reaching the wrong committee, or riding alone when
   they need to be inside a package?
9. Rates are set by the PSC case by case. Which parts of the citizens'
   transparency concern are about the PSC's process (a legislative-structure
   question) and which are about legislators themselves (an ethics-law
   question)?
