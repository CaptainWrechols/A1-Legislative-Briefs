Policy Spotlights — Housing Brief

A focused, bill-by-bill look at how far each proposal from the Community Conversations has traveled in Concord, 2020–2026. Each proposal’s record is sorted by how close its bills came to becoming law.

Fund the Housing Champions program and review eligibility criteria

The program exists, is funded through mid-2026, and survived a repeal vote — the open questions are renewal money and the designation criteria.

Already law

  • HB2 2023 (sections 79:462–466): created the voluntary Housing Champion designation and its grant programs, with $5 million plus $250,000 for administration — after SB145 (2023) had passed the Senate 21–3 as a standalone bill.
  • HB2 2025 (141:355, 141:214): extended the $5 million so it does not lapse until mid-2026 and widened the fund to pay for compiling public land suitable for housing.
  • HB1588 (2026): expanded the housing infrastructure municipal grant program with new money, alongside its zoning provisions.

Survived a repeal

  • HB1196 (2026): would have repealed the program; passed the House 185–166 with Republicans in favor and every voting Democrat opposed — the Senate killed it, and the program stands.

Stopped early

  • SB279 (2025): a Housing Champion business loan program; died at the Senate’s own end-of-year deadline.
  • SB419 (2026): Housing Champion changes plus Affordable Housing Fund appropriations; sent to interim study 16–8.
  • HB572 (2026): the standalone Partners in Housing bill was killed on the House floor 191–156 — after HB2 2025 (141:212) had already enacted the program.

Never filed

  • No bill adjusts how applicants are ranked — the one table’s idea of scoring high-density applicants higher has no legislative record. The designation criteria live in RSA 12-O:71–76, and every change so far has moved through the budget trailer rather than a standalone bill.

Increase density and use ADUs / creative & alternative housing types

The record’s busiest thread. Narrow, targeted versions now pass with cross-party votes; statewide unit mandates have failed four years running — and, on the concern that the state has little power over local zoning, the enacted laws are statewide rules that override local codes.

Already law

  • HB577 (2025): detached ADUs allowed by right and a larger maximum size (Chapter 197).
  • HB1079 (2026): ADUs in or attached to non-conforming structures (Chapter 210).
  • HB457 (2025): limits on how far local zoning can restrict dwelling units (Chapter 188).
  • HB631 (2025), HB1010 and HB1588 (2026): homes and multifamily buildings allowed on commercially zoned land, with parking-mandate limits and grant money (Chapters 201, 319, 329).
  • HB1400 (2024): office-to-housing conversion incentives, parking caps, alternative-parking rules, faster local zoning amendments (Chapter 370).
  • HB1361 (2024): municipalities must provide realistic opportunities for manufactured housing (Chapter 23).
  • HB1681 (2026): tiny houses and yurts legalized as innovative housing, 242–102 on concurrence (Chapter 330) — after tiny-house bills died in 2020, 2021, and 2022.

Proven support (cleared one chamber)

  • HB1291 (2024): two ADUs per lot by right; passed the House 220–143 (150 Democrats, 67 Republicans) and the Senate indefinitely postponed it after a rescue amendment failed 8–16.
  • HB1399 (2024): merely letting towns permit two units in certain single-family zones; passed the House 220–140 and sits in Senate interim study.
  • SB84 (2025): residential zoning procedures; passed the Senate 13–10, carried across the biennium, ended in interim study.

Stopped early

  • Unit mandates, in escalating losses: HB341 (2021) in committee, HB1177 (2022) on the table, HB44 (2023) crushed 117–232 on the House floor.
  • ADU financing: loan forgiveness (HB604, 2026) in interim study; loan guarantees (HB1662, 2026) died.
  • The most aggressive density versions: SB90 and HB1065 (2026), high-density residential on commercial land, both died.

Regulate corporations / private equity buying up housing; require them to pay more in taxes

Never filed as an ownership limit — no New Hampshire bill in the certified 2020–2026 record proposes capping corporate home purchases or registering investor owners. The tax side reached the House floor once and stayed on the table.

Stopped early (the tax side)

  • HB1786 (2026): a state assessment on luxury second homes to fund housing programs; tabled 189–158, and the motion to take it back up failed 100–235.
  • HB1707 (2026): a tax on certain unoccupied properties paired with a transfer-tax exemption for low- and moderate-income buyers; killed in the House.

Stopped early (corporate-landlord practices)

  • HB558 (2025): a public county rent registry plus a ban on algorithmic rent-setting; died in the House.
  • HB1612 (2026): the price-fixing-software ban on its own; laid on the table.
  • HB1368 (2024): would have barred eviction for nonpayment of corporate-raised rent; killed.

Never filed

  • A purchase cap, an investor registry, or a higher tax rate on corporate-owned housing. The one enacted study in the investment space points the other direction: HB633 (2025) created a committee studying housing investment trusts as a financing tool (Chapter 130).

Reverse the affordable / luxury ratio and build entry-level “starter” homes

No bill regulates the affordable-to-luxury mix of new construction, and no starter-home incentive has been filed. What exists in law attacks the supply side from other angles; the workforce-housing funding designs keep dying in the House.

Already law

  • Partners in Housing (HB2 2025, 141:212): state funding for workforce housing built on public land transferred to developers, with a required workforce-unit share.
  • Expedited review (HB2 2025, 141:213): fast-track site plan review for listed properties and for solely residential workforce-housing projects.
  • HB1065 (2024): extended the sprinkler exemption to three- and four-family homes — cutting the cost of exactly the buildings starter conversions produce (Chapter 324).
  • HB1681 (2026) and HB1400 (2024): tiny houses and office conversions — the smallest and cheapest new units in the record.

Stopped early

  • HB1764 (2026): community workforce housing targets, special assessments, and a revolving loan fund; killed on the House floor.
  • HB1661 (2026): expansion of New Hampshire Housing’s community heroes homebuying program; killed 179–166.
  • HB1101 (2024): a study of state loans for low-to-moderate income homebuyers; killed.
  • SB202 (2023): a homeownership innovations fund at New Hampshire Housing; died on the Senate table.
  • HB1660 (2026): tax-increment financing for priority housing development; died on its committee’s recommendation.

Defensive note

  • HB1619 (2026): would have repealed the workforce housing law entirely; the House sent it to interim study rather than passing it.

Never filed

  • Any bill directly requiring or incentivizing a share of entry-level homes in new construction.

Increase property taxes on second homes / restrict Airbnbs and vacation homes

Raised as an “other” proposal at the Conversations. The second-home tax reached the House floor in 2026 and stayed on the table; short-term rental restriction bills die in every form tried — and the one bill that moved ran the opposite way.

Stopped early

  • HB1786 (2026): the luxury second-home assessment (see above); tabled 189–158.
  • Short-term rental rules: a state STR registry (HB1498, 2024), an STR definition (HB1635, 2024), and municipal authority over vacation rentals (SB458, 2020) all died.
  • Occupancy fees on room rentals: HB1160 (2020) passed the House 174–113 and died on the Senate’s pandemic table; SB338 (2022) and SB262 (2023) died outright.
  • SB434 (2024): an extended-stay housing program with a tax exemption; killed in the Senate.

The opposite direction

  • SB249 (2022): would have barred towns from prohibiting short-term rentals; sent to interim study — the closest any STR bill came to moving.

Never filed

  • A statewide second-home property-tax rate. (Homestead-exemption designs, which shift burden toward non-primary homes, have circulated in the property-tax debate — a separate Forum issue.)
