Appendix B — Theme scorecards

Per-theme deep dives behind the front brief, with each theme’s history basket and a certainty label based on how many bills support the pattern. The baskets describe history only: “Often moved before” (similar ideas often became law), “Got support but didn’t finish” (real momentum, no enactment), “Rarely moved before” (repeated early stops).

Local tools to pay for growth (penny taxes, tolls, local fees)

  • Bills: 35 — Enacted: 4
  • Basket: Mixed — certainty: medium-high
  • The one genuinely new local tool passed: the County Green Space Sales Tax Act (2021-22 S152, Act 166 of 2022; Senate 43–1 and 41–3, House 67–28) — a voter-approved preservation penny.
  • Every bill widening the transportation penny died in first committee without a hearing: transit-eligibility clarifications filed in all four sessions (2019-20 S611/H4389, 2021-22 H3535/S437, 2023-24 S290/S562/H4059, 2025-26 S979), penny stacking (2021-22 H3129), eleven-year reimposition (2023-24 S116), grocery exemptions (2023-24 H5188/S1113).
  • All five I-95 toll bills died in first committee (2019-20 S178/H3739/S780, 2023-24 S499/S674), as did the local-option gas fee (2019-20 S172).
  • Flexibility acts passed at the edges: tourism taxes for flooding and drainage (2019-20 S217, Act 146, 34–2/87–15), beach-parking rules (2021-22 S40, Act 89, 44–0/102–10), the school-buildings penny opened to more counties (2025-26 H4589, Act 203, 81–18/40–2/91–14).

Making developers pay (impact fees)

  • Bills: 10 — Enacted: 0
  • Basket: Rarely moved before — certainty: high
  • Ten bills over three sessions, in both directions — expanding the 1999 Development Impact Fee Act (2023-24 H4659 residential-only fees, 2025-26 H5088 resurfacing as a fundable cost, 2021-22 H3460 and 2025-26 H4008 gentrification-fund fees, 2021-22 H4943 debt payoff) and narrowing it (2023-24 H4981/S856/H5017 and 2025-26 H3165 cost exclusions, 2025-26 H4672 repeat-buyer exemption).
  • Zero hearings, zero votes, either direction, eight years — Ways and Means, Finance, Judiciary, and the municipal-affairs committee.
  • The one enacted impact-fee rule blocked the fees: FY 2021-22 proviso 117.96 barred impact fees on new school construction for one year and was not renewed. No proffer bill has ever been filed.

State road money (gas tax, road fees, infrastructure funds)

  • Bills: 26 — Enacted: 2
  • Basket: Mixed — certainty: medium-high
  • Ten measures against the gas user fee (freeze 2021-22 H4091, repeal 2021-22 H4092, suspensions 2021-22 H5103/H5112 and 2025-26 S1045/H5398/H5419/H5422/H5443/H5475) all died in the money committees without votes — as did the one bill raising the county share (2025-26 H5331) and the EV-fee repeals (2021-22 H4945, 2023-24 H3177).

  • The infrastructure-bank restructurings — merging its board with SCDOT’s commission (2019-20 H3418) or abolishing it (2025-26 H4031) — died in Ways and Means.

  • Bills: 9 — Enacted: 0

Appendix F — What this data can and cannot say

This appendix states the limits of the record behind the brief, so readers know exactly how much weight each claim can carry.

Where the data comes from

  • Every bill, action, sponsor line, and floor vote comes from the South Carolina Legislature’s official website (scstatehouse.gov), collected as part of a certified sweep of all 15,817 instruments filed in the 123rd through 126th General Assemblies (2019–2026), plus every chamber roll call and ratification.
  • Budget provisos come from the enacted (“ratified”) Part IB text of each annual General Appropriations Act on the same official site. Dollar figures quoted for provisos are verbatim from that text.
  • The curated set was found by keyword discovery (official full-text search plus a local full-text and title scan of the certified universe) and then hand-reviewed bill by bill. It is a careful selection, not a proven-complete census of every growth-related bill. Nine bills were hand-added from the certified universe because the original search terms did not cover “sales tax”, “annexation”, or “hospitality tax” phrasings; a title scan for those phrasings was run across all four sessions.

What the data cannot say

  • No committee vote counts exist. South Carolina publishes committee outcomes (“Favorable”, “Favorable with amendment”) but never tally tables. Nothing in this packet states or implies a committee vote count, because none exists to cite.
  • No party labels. Per-member ballot PDFs list names only, and this packet does not include the member-roster join that would attach parties. The brief therefore makes no party claims at all.
  • Voice votes leave no numbers. Many bills — including some that became law — show few or no roll calls because South Carolina uses voice votes heavily. An empty vote history is a real answer, not missing data.
  • Committee silence has no recorded reason. When a bill dies in its first committee, the record shows no hearing, no vote, and no explanation. The brief reports the stop; it cannot report why.
  • Whole-bill votes only for provisos. Floor votes attach to the entire appropriations bill (or a named amendment), never to one proviso. No vote in this packet is presented as a vote on a specific proviso.
  • One-year rules. A proviso lasts one budget year unless re-adopted. Statements like “renewed every year” describe the observed 2021–2026 record, not a guarantee.
  • Background laws predate the record. The 2017 gas-tax act (Act 40) and the 1999 Development Impact Fee Act are described as background; they were enacted before this record’s 2019 start and are not part of the counts.
  • Dispositions are as of the collection date. For non-enacted 126th session (2025–2026) bills, “did not pass” reflects the record as of 2026-08-25, after the regular session’s final regular calendar.
  • Local County Transportation Committee bills routinely pass one chamber and finish through the county legislative delegation; their one-chamber passage is not a support signal for statewide policy and they are kept as context only.
  • The seven constituent proposals are Forum process input from the Phase 2 Community Conversations (June–August 2026), labeled [P-…]. They record what participants proposed — not verified facts, and not the Forum’s positions.

South Carolina enacts one-year policy rules — provisos — inside Part IB of each annual General Appropriations Act. A proviso lasts one budget year unless re-adopted, and floor votes attach to the whole budget bill (or a named amendment), never to a single proviso. Appendix H — The seven citizen proposals against the record

The Phase 2 Community Conversations (June–August 2026) produced seven growth-and-roads proposals. They are Forum process input — what participants proposed, at what frequency and consensus — not verified facts. This appendix matches each proposal against the 2019–2026 legislative record.

Source-document cross-check (2026-09-01): the frequency, tradeoff, and consensus descriptions below were verified against the “SC1 – Phase 2 Constituent Proposals – Grid View for Legislators v2” document. Six of the seven match it verbatim in substance. The exception is developer-pays-growth: its detail table (frequency/tradeoffs/consensus) is blank in the v2 document — the proposal itself appears in the document’s legislator-feedback grid, but its attributes here rest on the issue config’s proposal block alone. Flagged for human review.

[P-local-funding-tools] New/expanded local funding tools (penny sales tax, impact fees, tolls, parking fees)

  • What citizens said: give local governments new or expanded funding tools; raised in Columbia, Florence, and Charleston with high consensus. Transparency on how the funds are used was the condition participants attached.
  • What the record shows: the tools mostly exist — referendum-gated penny taxes for transportation (which can include tolls) and capital projects. Bills widening them (transit eligibility, stacking, longer terms) died unheard in the money committees in all four sessions; all five I-95 toll bills and the local-option gas fee died the same way. The one new tool enacted is the County Green Space Sales Tax (Act 166 of
    1. — for preservation, not roads. Flexibility acts passed at the edges (tourism-tax drainage repairs, beach parking, the school penny). The transparency condition is structurally present: every local tool requires a referendum whose ballot lists the projects.
  • Status: partially existing law; expansions rarely moved.

[P-developer-pays-growth] Make developers pay for growth (impact fees / proffers)

  • What citizens said: make developers pay for the infrastructure their projects create demand for; raised at all events, high consensus; pass-through to home prices the named concern.
  • What the record shows: impact fees have been legal since 1999, with strict limits (no maintenance funding). Ten bills fought over the Act in both directions — expansion and industry-side narrowing — and not one received a hearing in eight years. No proffer bill has ever been filed. The single enacted impact-fee rule blocked the fees (the FY 2021-22 school-construction prohibition, one year). The adjacent concurrency design died on the Senate floor calendar in 2026 after getting further than anything else in the lane.
  • Status: existing law; every change attempt rarely moved.

[P-multimodal-transport] Public transit, rail, and multimodal options beyond roads

  • What citizens said: invest in transit, rail, and multimodal options rather than roads alone; high frequency (3 events); high consensus in cities, rural relevance questioned.
  • What the record shows: zero hearings on every transit bill in four sessions — complete streets, transit-oriented development, developer transit stops, rail commissions, transit studies. The one rail measure with floor support moves freight (the shortline credit: House 106–3 and 65–46, dead in Senate Finance twice). The penny-tax transit clarifications died unheard too. Enacted at the edges: transit trespass (Act 222 of 2026) and the EV-charging framework (Act 46 of 2021).
  • Status: rarely moved; no hearings.

[P-state-master-planning] State comprehensive/regional planning instead of fragmented county plans

  • What citizens said: move toward state or regional planning for growth and infrastructure; raised at all four events; mixed consensus — the home-rule tension (local control versus state authority) runs through it, though it was adopted as a consensus recommendation at the Charleston conversation (process input).
  • What the record shows: the lever citizens described has never been filed — no state- or regional-planning bill exists in this record. What exists: one enacted state layer on local plans (the resilience act, Act 163 of 2020), the concurrency bills (S227 died at the last step in 2026), zoning-impact-assessment and annexation-control bills (all died in committee), and a developer-side counter-current pulling the opposite direction (also died). The one enacted state-over-local land-use rule is the 5G small-cell act (Act 179 of 2020).
  • Status: never filed (as described); adjacent designs mixed.

[P-fix-roads-first] Maintain existing roads before building new capacity

  • What citizens said: prioritize maintaining existing roads before expansion; medium frequency, mixed consensus.
  • What the record shows: every maintenance-accountability bill died in first committee (pavement preservation, transferred-roads maintenance, pothole hotlines, hazard reports). The idea is real in the budget: the yearly CTC fix-it-first rule (86.1), $417.4M (FY 2024-25), $200M (FY 2025-26), $225M (FY 2026-27) in one-time repair money, and the new Road Buyback Program — all one-year rules and one-time sums, not permanent law.
  • Status: bills rarely moved; money moved through the budget.

[P-no-new-taxes] Oppose new taxes or fees for infrastructure

  • What citizens said: fund infrastructure without new taxes or fees; medium frequency, low consensus — participants split sharply, and this proposal directly conflicts with local-funding-tools and developer-pays.
  • What the record shows: the no-new-taxes side is fully represented — ten measures to freeze, repeal, or suspend the gas fee — and every one died in the money committees without a vote, exactly like the new-tool bills. The one increase bill (the county gas-fee share) died in the same room. Where the two sides structurally meet: every local funding tool in the record is referendum-gated, so existing law already resolves the split by letting voters decide county by county.
  • Status: rarely moved (both directions).

[P-contractor-accountability] Improve the bidding process and hold contractors accountable

  • What citizens said: improve how road and infrastructure contracts are bid and hold contractors accountable for results; medium frequency, high consensus; seen as a low-cost fix.
  • What the record shows: design-build bidding died twice in committee; SCDOT’s own contractor performance-evaluation and disqualification regulations were stranded on the 2020 Senate calendar; expenditure-report certification died in Ways and Means. What exists today is proviso-based transparency: published project priority lists with ranking methodology (renewed yearly) and the public project dashboard directive with forecast-versus-actual costs (FY 2024-25 onward) — one-year rules that lapse if a budget drops them. Edges passed as law: utility-relocation costs (Act 36 of 2019, extended by Act 244 of 2026) and contractor licensing (Act 69 of 2023).
  • Status: bills rarely moved; transparency tools exist as provisos.
