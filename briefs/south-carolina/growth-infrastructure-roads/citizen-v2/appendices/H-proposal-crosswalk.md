# Appendix H — The five citizen proposals against the record

The Phase 2 Community Conversations (June–August 2026) produced five
growth-and-roads proposals, per the final proposal grid ("SC1 - Growth
Infrastructure Roads", received 2026-09-04). They are Forum process input —
what participants proposed, at what frequency and consensus — not verified
facts. This appendix matches each proposal against the 2019–2026
legislative record.

Provenance note: the final grid supersedes the seven-proposal draft grid
("SC1 – Phase 2 Constituent Proposals – Grid View for Legislators v2") this
issue's config originally carried. The draft's standalone developer-pays
proposal does not appear in the final grid — its subject, impact fees, is
named inside the funding-tools proposal and the impact-fee record is
presented there. The draft's multimodal-transport proposal was also
dropped; the transit and rail legislative record remains in Appendices A–C
under its theme, without a proposal label.

## [P-local-funding-tools] New or expanded local funding tools (penny / sales tax, impact fees, tolls, parking fees)

- **What citizens said (final grid):** give local governments new or
  expanded funding tools. Frequency High — Columbia, Florence, and
  Charleston. Consensus High — broad agreement that the state needs to look
  for new sources of funding. Tradeoff/concern: people don't like being
  taxed if they don't know where the money is going — participants attached
  the condition that any taxes come with greater transparency.
- **What the record shows — the tax tools:** the tools mostly exist —
  referendum-gated penny taxes for transportation (which can include
  tolls) and capital projects. Bills widening them (transit eligibility,
  stacking, longer terms, grocery carve-outs) died unheard in the money
  committees in all four sessions; all five I-95 toll bills and the
  local-option gas fee died the same way. The one new tool enacted is the
  County Green Space Sales Tax (Act 166 of 2022) — for preservation, not
  roads. Flexibility acts passed at the edges (tourism-tax drainage
  repairs, beach parking, the school penny), and the SCDOT Modernization
  Act (Act 177 of 2026) rewrote the state toll framework: usage charges
  only on new-capacity "choice lanes."
- **What the record shows — the impact-fee tool:** legal since 1999 (the
  Development Impact Fee Act, Section 6-1-910 et seq.) with strict limits
  (no maintenance funding). Ten bills fought over the Act in both
  directions — expansion and industry-side narrowing — and not one
  received a hearing in eight years. The single enacted impact-fee rule
  blocked the fees (the FY 2021-22 school-construction prohibition, one
  year, not renewed).
- **The transparency condition:** structurally present in existing law —
  every local tax tool here requires a referendum whose ballot question
  lists the projects the money will fund.
- **Status:** tools partially existing law; expansions of both tools
  rarely moved.

## [P-state-master-planning] State comprehensive / master planning vs. fragmented county plans; regional control

- **What citizens said (final grid):** state comprehensive/master planning
  instead of fragmented county plans; regional control. Frequency Very
  High — all four community conversations. Consensus Mixed — some cited
  the efficiency gains from centralized planning while others remained
  skeptical of removing local control; it was a consensus recommendation
  in Charleston. Tradeoff/concern: removing local control could allow the
  state to go against residents' wishes.
- **What the record shows:** the lever citizens described has never been
  filed — no state- or regional-planning bill exists in this record. What
  exists: one enacted state layer on local plans (the resilience act,
  Act 163 of 2020), the concurrency bills (S227 died at the last step in
  2026), zoning-impact-assessment and annexation-control bills (all died in
  committee), and a developer-side counter-current pulling the opposite
  direction (also died). The one enacted state-over-local land-use rule is
  the 5G small-cell act (Act 179 of 2020). The record mirrors the
  tradeoff citizens named: bills pulling toward state authority and bills
  pulling toward local/developer flexibility died in the same committees.
- **Status:** never filed (as described); adjacent designs mixed.

## [P-fix-roads-first] Fix the roads we've got first

- **What citizens said (final grid):** prioritize the roads we already
  have. Frequency Medium — Columbia and the online session. Consensus
  Mixed — broad agreement that maintenance needs to be factored in, but
  many participants want to see expanded infrastructure. Tradeoff/concern:
  the expense of expansion versus maintaining what is already there; some
  worried the state was putting too much money toward capital projects and
  not enough toward maintenance.
- **What the record shows:** every standalone maintenance bill died in
  first committee (pavement preservation, transferred-roads maintenance,
  pothole hotlines, hazard reports) — but the pothole-reporting design
  became permanent law inside the SCDOT Modernization Act (Act 177 of
  2026): the Pothole Mitigation Program, with public reporting, a
  seven-day repair requirement, and $15 million a year for repeat
  potholes. The rest of the idea is real in the budget: the yearly CTC
  fix-it-first rule (86.1), $417.4M (FY 2024-25), $200M (FY 2025-26),
  $225M (FY 2026-27) in one-time repair money, and the new Road Buyback
  Program — one-year rules and one-time sums, not permanent law. The
  maintenance-versus-capital worry citizens raised maps directly onto that
  structure: the recurring maintenance rules live in one-year provisos
  while the big sums arrive as one-time capital-style transfers.
- **Status:** standalone bills rarely moved; one piece enacted inside
  Act 177; money moved through the budget.

## [P-no-new-taxes] No new taxes / oppose extra taxes and fees

- **What citizens said (final grid):** fund infrastructure without new
  taxes or fees. Frequency Medium — multiple tables at Columbia and
  Florence. Consensus Low — sharp disagreement over whether new taxes are
  essential for improvements or whether keeping costs low is the priority. Tradeoff/concern: some participants argued South Carolina's low
  taxes are one reason infrastructure is suffering, while others want to
  maintain the state's low taxes.
- **What the record shows:** the no-new-taxes side is fully represented —
  ten measures to freeze, repeal, or suspend the gas fee — and every one
  died in the money committees without a vote, exactly like the new-tool
  bills. The one increase bill (the county gas-fee share) died in the same
  room. The record mirrors the sharp split citizens showed: neither
  direction has had a hearing. Where the two sides structurally meet:
  every local funding tool in the record is referendum-gated, so existing
  law already resolves the split by letting voters decide county by
  county.
- **Status:** rarely moved (both directions).

## [P-contractor-accountability] Improve the contractor bidding process / hold contractors accountable

- **What citizens said (final grid):** improve how road and infrastructure
  contracts are bid and hold contractors accountable. Frequency Medium —
  the online conversation and Greenville. Consensus High — participants
  widely agreed this could be a low-cost fix. Tradeoff/concern:
  participants felt the state was not getting quality contractors for road
  repairs and pushed for more standards and transparency in bidding.
- **What the record shows:** the standalone design-build bills died twice
  in committee — and the method then passed inside the SCDOT Modernization
  Act (Act 177 of 2026), which gave SCDOT phased design-build and
  construction manager/general contractor authority, capped public-private
  partnership agreements at sixty years, and requires an independent
  external audit of SCDOT every four years. SCDOT's own contractor
  performance-evaluation and disqualification regulations were stranded on
  the 2020 Senate calendar; expenditure-report certification died in Ways
  and Means. What exists beyond Act 177 is proviso-based transparency:
  published project priority lists with ranking methodology (renewed
  yearly) and the public project dashboard directive with
  forecast-versus-actual costs (FY 2024-25 onward) — one-year rules that
  lapse if a budget drops them. Edges passed as law: utility-relocation
  costs (Act 36 of 2019, extended by Act 244 of 2026) and contractor
  licensing (Act 69 of 2023).
- **Status:** standalone bills rarely moved; the bidding method and audit
  were enacted inside Act 177; transparency tools exist as provisos.
