# Housing Affordability in Nevada

The legislative record behind Nevadans' ten most common housing proposals, 2019–2025 — organized by how close each idea has come to becoming law.

## What this brief covers

Nevada's Legislature took up 149 housing bills across its 2019–2025 sessions, and 63 became law — in two clear patterns. Supply and money bills now pass — in 2025 the ADU and by-right density mandates became law, and the $133 million attainable-housing account passed 42–0 in the Assembly. Renter-cost and ownership bills win floor majorities and stop at the last step: the governor's desk since 2023 — and once, in November 2025, one vote short of two-thirds.

## Closest to law: bills that passed both chambers

*These bills passed both houses; a veto, a missing signature, or a two-thirds rule stopped them.*

### Vetoed at the governor's desk

Fifteen of sixteen vetoes fall in 2023 and 2025 — nine in 2023, six in 2025 — nearly all on bills about renter costs and home ownership. **Limits on corporate ownership:** SB395 (2023) capped corporate and LLC home purchases at 1,000 a year and passed 14–6 and 28–14 — vetoed. **Rent caps for manufactured-home parks:** SB275 (2023), aimed at seniors on fixed incomes, passed 13–8 and 28–14 — vetoed. **Local power to require affordable housing:** SB371 (2023), confirming local governments may pass any affordable-housing rule not expressly barred by state law, passed 12–9 and 26–14 — vetoed. **Fee protections:** application-fee refunds with a plain-language fee list passed 36–6 as AB298 (2023) and again as AB280 (2025) — vetoed both times; the deposits-and-fees bill SB78 (2023) met the same end. AB218, the 2023 all-in pricing bill, was vetoed — then became law as AB121 in 2025: vetoed ideas can return and pass.

### Passed both chambers, then stalled at the final step

**Developer linkage fees — SB99 (2025).** Let the two big metros charge home developers a fee dedicated to affordable housing; it passed 14–6 and 27–15, reached the governor on May 27, 2025, and the record ends there: never signed, never law. **The corporate-purchase cap — SB10 (November 2025 special session).** A 1,000-home cap plus an investor registry passed the Senate 18–0 and won the Assembly 27–10 — one vote short of the required 28, and the strongest showing this idea has made.

## Proven support: cleared a chamber or a committee

*Each bill won a committee or floor majority before stalling.*

**Corporate-cap retry — SB391 (2025).** Cut the cap to 100 homes a year; passed the Senate 13–8 and died in the Assembly.

**First-home rate buy-down — SB193 (2025).** Ordered a state pilot lowering mortgage rates for eligible first-home borrowers; passed the Senate 14–5 and died in the Assembly. Existing buyer programs run through the Housing Division's bond authority, not statute.

**Housing Liaison — 2025.** Upgrading the Housing Division's Housing Advocate into a Housing Liaison passed the Assembly 42–0 and died in the Senate at the deadline; the ADU property-tax break for voucher-holder units died the same way in 2025, after a 42–0 Assembly vote, having failed in 2023. Unanimous Assembly support twice shows broad backing; the bills simply never got a Senate vote.

## Already law — and the openings around it

*New law settles some ground — and shows where the Legislature keeps building.*

### New laws from the 2025 session

AB540 created the $133 million Nevada Attainable Housing Account, 42–0 in the Assembly. AB396 requires Clark, Washoe, and the five largest cities to allow accessory dwelling units; AB241 requires apartments and mixed-use housing by right on commercial land — laws like these did not exist in Nevada before 2025. AB121 made advertised rent an all-in figure with fee disclosure. AB211 lets cities force repairs at neglected apartment properties, 41–0. AB475 funded eviction diversion in Clark County, Reno, and rural Nevada, 42–0 and 19–1. AB457 launched the study of corporate rental-owner taxation, reporting for 2027, and the special session revised the Windsor Park relocation act (SB6). Bills restating these would duplicate just-completed work and are unlikely to advance.

### Where lawmakers appear willing to go further

Fee-transparency laws are the Legislature's most consistent housing achievement, each new law building on the last: the 5 percent late-fee cap (SB151, 2019), the three-day grace period (AB308, 2021), the unanimous ban on charging tenants for the landlord's own repairs (SB381, 2023), and all-in pricing (AB121, 2025); application-fee refunds, still unfinished, passed twice and were vetoed twice. On supply, SB150 (2021) legalized tiny houses in some zones and AB38 (2025) modernized the factory-built housing code, 40–2 and 17–3; the incentive side — SB430 (2025), tax credits for builders of at-cost starter homes — died in committee without a vote, untested rather than rejected. A builders–citizens–legislators working group already exists in law: AB476 (2019), carried by a cross-party team, recreated the Advisory Committee on Housing (annual needs report, one bill draft per session), 38–2 and 20–0; study bills remain the most reliably enacted housing bills. SB188 (2021) created donor-funded matched-savings accounts toward a first home after the same idea died in 2019.

## Little traction yet: never filed or stopped early

*No votes yet: two of the ten citizen proposals were never filed; the rest stopped early.*

**Inclusionary requirements.** No bill has ever required developments to include a share of affordable units. Every filed bill sits one step away — local authority to require it (SB371, vetoed; the 2025 retry SB289 died in Senate Government Affairs); the pay-instead-of-build funds (AB331, AB334, 2021) died in their first committee.

**Loan forgiveness and homebuying help for high-demand professions.** Never filed. The model is already in law — the health-provider loan-repayment program, expanded twice in 2025 (AB269, SB266) — and the closest attempts stopped short: SB47 (2023), letting school districts house their own staff, died on the Senate floor; the 2025 buy-down pilot selects by income, not profession.

**Rent caps and their mirror image.** The general cap died twice in 2023 without an Assembly floor vote (AB362 in committee, SB426 on the Senate floor); the 2025 manufactured-home retry and the bills banning local rent control (SB123, AB443, 2025) all died in their first committees. Nevada law neither caps rents nor stops cities from trying; the one cap that passed, SB275, was vetoed.

**Registries and impact fees.** Statewide landlord registries died in first committee in 2021 and 2025; school impact fees (SB471, 2019) and developer in-lieu funds (2021) died in Government Affairs. The one law in this space runs the other way: SB103 (2019) lets local governments reduce fees for affordable-housing projects.

## Where there is movement — and where there is none


**Moving:** supply and money (mandates plus a $133 million account in 2025); fee transparency (a new law almost every session since 2019); corporate-ownership limits (majorities in both chambers in 2023, the Senate in 2025, a one-vote special-session miss, a 2027 study — the most movement of any unfinished idea). **Not moving:** rent caps (the cap and the cap-ban both die early); inclusionary mandates and profession-based homebuyer help (never filed); landlord registries and impact fees (repeated first-committee deaths).

**Where bills stop, and who sponsors them.** Rent and landlord-tenant bills die first in Commerce and Labor (11 first-committee stops); local-power, fee, and registry bills in Government Affairs (14). Cross-party teams appeared on only 16 policy bills — 13 became law, the best odds in the record. Senator Julia Ratti (D), who wrote the 2019 wave of renter-protection laws, has left; Senator Dina Neal (D), who sponsors the corporate-cap bills, still serves. Since 2023, the governor's signature or veto has decided these bills.

## Federal overlap: what Washington already covers

*Where federal action already covers ground — and where the state route is the only one.*

No federal law caps rents or limits corporate home purchases — congressional proposals to curb hedge-fund ownership were introduced, not passed — so Nevada's corporate-cap and rent bills do not duplicate any federal law. Federal supply money runs through the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit and HUD block grants; AB540 adds to rather than repeats them. FHA and VA loans serve first-time buyers, but federal down-payment tax credits have not passed, so state pilots like SB193 would not duplicate a federal program. The 2024 federal junk-fee rule covers hotels and tickets, not rental housing — AB121 and the vetoed refund bills are the only all-in-pricing rules for Nevada renters.

## Proposal spotlights

## Limiting corporate ownership of housing

**Support has grown every time it was tested — and the two-thirds rule, not the cap, decided the last vote.** SB395 (2023), a 1,000-home annual cap, passed 14–6 (one Republican joining) and 28–14, and was vetoed; SB391 (2025), a 100-home cap, passed the Senate 13–8 and died in the Assembly. In the November 2025 special session, SB10 — the cap plus an investor registry — passed the Senate 18–0, every senator of both parties, and won the Assembly 27–10; its registry fees made it a revenue measure needing 28 votes. A cap without fees would need only a simple majority — and the governor's signature. AB457's corporate-landlord tax study reports for 2027, and Senator Dina Neal (D), the idea's most consistent sponsor, still serves.

## Zoning reform and smaller, denser homes

**Requirements passed on party-line votes; building-code updates passed with both parties; incentives remain untested.** The 2025 mandates became law narrowly — AB396 (accessory dwelling units) 27–15 and 14–7, AB241 (apartments by right on commercial land) 28–14 and 15–6, one Republican vote in most chambers — while the building-code updates drew both parties: AB38 (2025) rewrote the factory-built housing code 40–2 and 17–3, and SB150 (2021) legalized tiny houses in some zones. The incentive side, SB430 (2025) — tax credits for at-cost starter homes with investor-resale protections — died in Senate Revenue without a vote; no committee has yet voted on a small-home incentive.

## Affordable-housing shares and in-lieu funds

**The requirement itself has never been filed; every related bill stopped in a Government Affairs committee or at the governor's desk.** Local authority to impose such conditions passed both chambers (SB371, 2023, 12–9 and 26–14) and was vetoed; the 2025 retry, SB289, died in Senate Government Affairs. The fund version reached the desk as SB99 (2025), the metro linkage fee, 14–6 and 27–15 — delivered to the governor on May 27, 2025, and never signed into law. In-lieu funds (AB331, AB334, 2021) and school impact fees (SB471, 2019) died in Government Affairs. The one enacted bill runs the other way: SB103 (2019) lets local governments reduce fees for affordable projects.

## Down-payment help for first-time buyers

**The one housing idea with real two-party Senate support — and an Assembly that has never voted on it.** SB193 (2025), the state rate buy-down pilot for first-home borrowers, passed the Senate 14–5 with three Republicans and died in the Assembly without a vote. The enacted precedent, SB188 (2021), created donor-matched savings accounts toward a first home after the same idea failed in 2019. Standing buyer programs run through the Housing Division's bond authority, not statute; federally, FHA and VA loans lower down-payment barriers while proposed federal down-payment credits remain unenacted — a state program supplements rather than duplicates.

## Glossary

- **Accessory dwelling unit (ADU):** A small second home on the same lot as a main house — a casita, garage apartment, or backyard cottage. Nevada's largest jurisdictions must now allow them.
- **Advisory Committee on Housing:** The statutory advisory body of builders, citizens, and legislators, with an annual statewide needs report and the right to request one bill draft per session.
- **All-in pricing:** Requiring the advertised rent to be the real total, with mandatory fees included and disclosed — now law.
- **Application fee / late fee / grace period:** The fee to apply for a rental; the penalty for late rent (capped at 5 percent); and the three-day window after the due date before a late fee may be charged.
- **At-cost starter homes / resale protections:** Homes sold at construction cost to moderate-income buyers, with rules keeping them from being flipped to investors — an untested design.
- **Attainable housing:** Housing affordable to working households; Nevada's new $133 million account funds it.
- **By right:** Development a property owner may build without asking for special permission — no rezoning, no discretionary vote. Apartments on commercial land are now by right statewide.
- **Corporate-purchase cap:** An annual limit on how many homes corporations and LLCs may buy — the record's most-advanced unfinished idea.
- **Eviction diversion:** Programs that resolve nonpayment cases — through mediation or rental assistance — before they become evictions.
- **FHA / VA loans:** Federally backed mortgages — insured by the Federal Housing Administration or guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs — that let buyers qualify with smaller down payments.
- **Hedge fund:** An investment fund pooling money from large investors — shorthand in the corporate-ownership debate for institutional buyers of single-family homes.
- **Housing Advocate / Housing Liaison:** The Housing Division's public-facing ombudsman role, and the proposed upgrade of it.
- **Housing Division / bond authority:** The state housing agency, which finances homebuyer programs by issuing bonds rather than through session-by-session laws.
- **HUD block grants:** Flexible federal housing and community-development money sent to states and cities by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development.
- **Impact fee:** A charge on new development to fund the infrastructure it will need — roads, sewers, schools.
- **Inclusionary requirement:** A mandate that new developments include a percentage of affordable units — never filed in Nevada.
- **In-lieu fund / in-lieu fee:** Letting a developer pay into a housing fund instead of building required affordable units.
- **Investor registry:** A required state register of investor-owned rental homes.
- **Junk-fee rule:** The 2024 federal rule requiring up-front disclosure of mandatory fees — it covers lodging and event tickets, not rental housing.
- **Landlord registry:** A proposed statewide list of rental-property owners.
- **Linkage fee:** A charge on new residential development, dedicated to affordable housing.
- **LLC:** Limited liability company — the business structure many investor landlords use.
- **Low-Income Housing Tax Credit:** The main federal subsidy for building affordable rentals — tax credits investors buy, financing the projects.
- **Manufactured-home park:** A community of factory-built homes whose owners typically rent the lot under them — why the one rent cap that passed targeted parks and the seniors living in them.
- **Matched-savings accounts:** Savings accounts where donors match what a family deposits toward a first home.
- **Rate buy-down:** Paying money up front to lower a mortgage's interest rate; a state pilot for first-home borrowers passed the Senate and stalled.
- **Rent cap / rent control:** A legal limit on how much rent can rise; "the cap-ban" refers to mirror-image bills that would forbid cities from adopting any rent control.
- **Tiny house / factory-built housing:** Very small homes, and homes built in factories and assembled on site — both now recognized in Nevada law.
- **Voucher holders:** Renters who pay part of their rent with a federal housing voucher (often called Section 8).
- **Windsor Park relocation act:** The state law relocating residents of Windsor Park, a North Las Vegas neighborhood where sinking ground has damaged homes.

## Legislative process glossary

- **AB / SB:** Assembly Bill / Senate Bill — the chamber a bill started in, plus its number. Numbering restarts every session, so the same number in different years is a different bill.
- **Amendment / concurrence:** A change made to a bill along the way; if one chamber amends, the other must vote to accept — "concur in" — the change. Bills can die awaiting that one last vote.
- **Appropriation:** A bill or provision that sets aside state money for a purpose.
- **Chambers:** The Legislature's two houses: the Assembly (42 members) and the Senate (21). A bill must pass both to reach the governor.
- **Committee / first committee:** A small panel of lawmakers that studies a bill and votes on whether to send it onward; most bills that fail die in their first committee — "never heard" means no hearing at all.
- **D / R:** Democrat / Republican, shown after a legislator's name; "still serves" and "has left" reflect the current roster.
- **Delivered / signed / chapter:** A passed bill is delivered to the governor; a signed bill becomes a numbered "chapter" of state law, and "no signature, no chapter" means it never became law.
- **Died at session's end / out of time:** A bill that cleared every vote taken but ran out of calendar before the final step — in these briefs, a mark of proven support, not rejection.
- **Floor vote / floor calendar:** A vote of the full chamber, and the schedule of bills waiting for one; a bill "dies on the calendar" when the session ends before its turn — a scheduling death, not a defeat.
- **Interim committee / interim study:** Panels that meet between sessions to study a topic and prepare recommendations for the next session.
- **Money committees / the money stage:** Assembly Ways and Means and Senate Finance review anything that costs money; bills with price tags routinely stop there without a vote against the policy itself.
- **Second house:** After passing the chamber where it started, a bill repeats the entire process — committee, then floor — in the other chamber.
- **Session:** The Legislature meets for 120 days in odd-numbered years (2019, 2021, 2023, 2025); a bill that has not finished every step by then is dead and must start over in a future session.
- **Special session:** A short extra meeting the governor calls for specific topics; bills that ran out of regular-session time can pass there.
- **Sponsor:** The lawmaker or committee that formally introduces a bill; a committee-sponsored bill has no single author. Cross-party sponsorship — sponsors from both parties — is one of the strongest signals a bill will pass.
- **Statute / enacted:** A statute is a law on the books; an enacted bill is one that became law. "By rule, not statute" means an agency or local authority adopted something the Legislature has not.
- **Two-thirds majority:** The constitution requires a two-thirds vote in each chamber — 28 of 42, 14 of 21 — for any measure raising revenue, including fees; a bill can win a majority and still fail this bar.
- **Veto:** The governor's rejection of a bill that passed both chambers. A vetoed bill does not become law unless two-thirds of each chamber votes to override the veto.
- **Vote pairs such as "42–0 and 21–0":** Floor votes are yes–no tallies, one per chamber; 42–0 is a unanimous Assembly vote, 21–0 a unanimous Senate vote.
