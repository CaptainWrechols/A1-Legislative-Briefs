# Appendix H — Citizen statements, checked against the record

The ten most common citizen statements on housing from the Phase 2 Community Conversations (RAG constituent-voice dataset), each checked against the 2019–2025 bill record. "Never filed" means no bill in this record proposed it — not that it is impossible.


## 1. Limit corporate/institutional ownership of housing - cap how many properties one owner can hold.

- **Status:** Tried; stopped at the finish line
- **The record:** The most-advanced idea in the set that is still not law. 2023 SB395 (Sen. Dina Neal, D) capped corporate/LLC purchases at 1,000 homes a year statewide, passed the Senate 14-6 and the Assembly 28-14, and was vetoed. 2025 SB391 cut the cap to 100 homes, passed the Senate 13-8, and died in the Assembly without a vote. In the 36th (2025) Special Session, SB10 (Sen. Cannizzaro) - a 1,000-unit cap plus a corporate-investor registry - passed the Senate 18-0 and then lost the Assembly final-passage vote 27-10: the bill's registration-fee provisions required a two-thirds majority (28 of 42), so a 27-vote majority was recorded as Lost. This is the bill participants remembered from 'the last special session.' Related: 2023 AB345 (end the REIT commerce-tax exemption) passed the Assembly 28-13 and died in the Senate; 2021 SB159 (owner-occupant priority at foreclosure auctions, no bulk sales) died in Senate Judiciary. What is law: 2025 AB457 orders an interim study of taxing corporate rental-property owners as single entities, reporting for 2027.
- **Where the decision sits:** Fully a state-legislature lever; the caps regulate purchases statewide. The two-thirds requirement is constitutional and attaches to the fee/registry design, not to the cap itself.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 SB395 | Capped at 1,000 the homes corporations and LLCs together may buy per year statewide | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2025 SB391 | Capped at 100 the homes corporations and LLCs together may buy per year statewide | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2023 AB345 | Ended the commerce-tax exemption for real estate investment trusts | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2021 SB159 | Gave owner-occupants and tenant buyers priority over investors at foreclosure auctions and banned bulk sales | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2025 AB457 | Ordered an interim study of taxing corporate rental-property owners as single entities and taxing controlling-interest transfers | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Would a cap without a fee-funded registry avoid the two-thirds requirement, and what enforcement would it lose?
- What did the 2023 veto message object to, and does the 100-unit versus 1,000-unit threshold change that?
- The AB457 tax study reports for 2027 - does a tax approach substitute for a cap or complement it?

## 2. Down payment assistance / first-time buyer programs, with a streamlined approval process.

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** The direct state attempt is recent and died late: 2025 SB193 (Sen. Neal) ordered the Housing Division to run a pilot buying down mortgage interest rates to roughly 4 percent for eligible first-home borrowers; it passed the Senate 14-5 and died in the Assembly without a floor vote. What is law is adjacent: 2021 SB188 created the matched-savings Individual Development Account program (donor-funded, savings matched up to 5-to-1, usable toward first homes) after the same idea died on the Senate floor in 2019 (SB194). The Housing Division already holds bond authority to lower borrowing costs (the hook SB193 built on), which is where Nevada's existing homebuyer programs live - in agency practice, not session-by-session statute.
- **Where the decision sits:** Split lever: the Legislature can order and fund programs, but day-to-day down-payment assistance runs through the Housing Division's bond programs; loan-approval standards are federal/lender territory, which no bill in this record touches.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 SB193 | State pilot to buy down mortgage interest rates for eligible first-home borrowers | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2021 SB188 | Created the matched-savings Individual Development Account Program, usable toward housing goals | Enacted | Became law |
| 2019 SB194 | Matched-savings accounts (IDAs) low-income families can use toward goals like a home | Failed | Died on its first floor / calendar |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Why did SB193 stall in the Assembly after a 14-5 Senate vote - money committee scoring or calendar?
- Does the IDA program's donor-funded design (no general-fund money) explain why it passed when SB193's state-funded pilot did not?

## 3. Require developers to fund infrastructure / 'pay to play' rather than receive incentives; a state fund for affordable housing built from required contributions.

- **Status:** Tried; stopped at the finish line
- **The record:** Citizens described almost exactly 2025 SB99: it authorized cities and counties in the two big metros to charge residential developers a linkage fee dedicated to affordable housing. It passed the Senate 14-6 and the Assembly 27-15, was enrolled and delivered to the governor on May 27, 2025 - and the official history ends there, with no signature and no chapter number. It is not law. The earlier, quieter versions died in first committee: 2021 AB331 and AB334 (developer in-lieu payments into an affordable-housing trust fund) both died in Assembly Government Affairs; 2019 SB471 (impact fees for school construction) died in Senate Government Affairs. The one enacted bill in this space points the other way: 2019 SB103 lets local governments reduce or subsidize impact and permit fees for affordable-housing projects (21-0, 36-4). Nevada's impact-fee statute (NRS 278B) already lets local governments charge developers for certain capital improvements - the fights above are about expanding what counts.
- **Where the decision sits:** Mixed lever: impact fees are charged locally under state authorization, so the statewide fight is over what the Legislature permits localities to charge; a mandatory state-level developer contribution has never been filed.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 SB99 | Let cities and counties charge developers a linkage fee to fund affordable housing | Failed | Passed both houses, still no law |
| 2021 AB331 | Let local governments take developer in-lieu payments into a fund for affordable housing obligations | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 AB334 | Let local governments take developer in-lieu payments into a fund for affordable housing obligations | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2019 SB471 | Let local governments charge developer impact fees for school construction | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2019 SB103 | Let local governments cut or subsidize impact and permit fees for affordable housing projects | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- What happened to SB99 at the desk - the record shows delivery and then nothing; the veto/no-signature distinction matters for a retry.
- Would participants' state infrastructure-bank design need new machinery, or can the linkage-fee authorization plus existing local tools carry it?

## 4. Inclusionary requirements - mandate a percentage of affordable units per development.

- **Status:** Never filed
- **The record:** No bill in this record mandates a percentage of affordable units in private developments, and none imposes a statewide inclusionary requirement. The real fight has been one step earlier - whether local governments may impose such conditions at all: 2023 SB371, which confirmed local power to enact any affordable-housing ordinance not expressly barred by statute, passed the Senate 12-9 and the Assembly 26-14 and was vetoed; the 2025 retry (SB289) died in Senate Government Affairs. The nearest filed designs are the pay-instead-of-build versions: developer in-lieu contributions (2021 AB331/AB334, died in first committee) and the 2025 linkage fee (SB99, died unsigned at the desk).
- **Where the decision sits:** Today this is a local zoning question operating under state authorization; the vetoed SB371 record shows the Legislature and governor disagree about how much room localities have. A statewide mandate would be new ground.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 SB371 | Confirmed local governments can enact any affordable-housing ordinance not expressly barred | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2025 SB289 | Confirmed local governments can enact any affordable-housing ordinance not expressly barred | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 AB331 | Let local governments take developer in-lieu payments into a fund for affordable housing obligations | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 AB334 | Let local governments take developer in-lieu payments into a fund for affordable housing obligations | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2025 SB99 | Let cities and counties charge developers a linkage fee to fund affordable housing | Failed | Passed both houses, still no law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Do Nevada localities believe they already have inclusionary authority, or is the SB371 fight the blocker?
- Participants split on incentive versus mandate - the record only tests the authority question, not either design.

## 5. Rent regulation / guardrails on rent increases - deliberately not called 'rent control.'

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** Participants' prediction that rent control 'will get shut down quickly' matches this record precisely - in both directions. The general guardrail design (no increases in year one; annual increases capped at a published cost-of-living rate) was filed twice in 2023: AB362 died in Assembly Commerce and Labor, SB426 died on the Senate floor. The one rent cap that passed - 2023 SB275, capping annual rent increases in manufactured home parks, aimed at seniors on fixed incomes - passed the Senate 13-8 and the Assembly 28-14 and was vetoed; the 2025 retry (SB151) died in its first committee. The mirror-image bills also failed: 2025 SB123 (ban local rent control) died in Senate Government Affairs, and 2025 AB443 (ban rent control and strip local zoning powers) died in Assembly Commerce and Labor. Nevada law today neither caps rents nor forbids localities from trying.
- **Where the decision sits:** State lever with a local layer: nothing in current statute stops a city or county from attempting rent regulation, and nothing authorizes it explicitly - both clarifying bills died.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 AB362 | Capped annual rent increases for existing tenants at a published cost-of-living rate | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2023 SB426 | Capped annual rent increases for existing tenants at a published cost-of-living rate | Failed | Died on its first floor / calendar |
| 2023 SB275 | Capped annual rent increases in manufactured home parks at a published percentage | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2025 SB151 | Capped annual rent increases in manufactured home parks at a published percentage | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2025 SB123 | Banned counties and cities from enacting rent control | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2025 AB443 | Banned local rent control and stripped local zoning powers, moving development rules to the state | Failed | Died in its first committee |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- The senior-focused park cap is the version that got furthest - is a narrower, population-scoped guardrail the tested path?
- What did the SB275 veto object to - the cap mechanics or the principle?

## 6. Regulate/cap rental fees and improve fee transparency.

- **Status:** Passed repeatedly, vetoed repeatedly; precedent exists
- **The record:** Participants remembered that 'the legislature has already put caps on certain fees' - true, and the rest of the story is the veto wall. In statute now: the 5 percent late-fee cap (2019 SB151, part of the summary-eviction rewrite), a 3-day grace period before late fees (2021 AB308), a ban on charging tenants fees for repairs the landlord must make anyway (2023 SB381 - unanimous, 21-0 and 42-0), and all-in advertised rent pricing with fee disclosure (2025 AB121). Vetoed: 2023 AB298 (refund application fees when no unit is available, plus a plain-language appendix of every fee - passed 36-6 and 12-8), its 2025 retry AB280 (27-15, 13-8), 2023 AB218 (the all-in pricing bill that later passed as AB121), and 2023 SB78 (security deposits and prospective-tenant fees, 28-14 and 14-7). Died earlier: 2019 SB256 (late-fee caps, 11-10 Senate, died in the Assembly), 2021 SB218 (12-9, same), and both statewide landlord registries with rent reporting (2021 AB332, 2025 SB436, each dead in first committee).
- **Where the decision sits:** Fully a state-legislature lever (NRS 118A). Enforcement capacity - the gap participants flagged - is the part no enacted bill funds.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 SB151 | Rewrote the summary eviction process for renters, with longer notice for late rent | Enacted | Became law |
| 2021 AB308 | Added a 3-day grace period before late-rent fees and clarified rent-increase notices | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 SB381 | Banned charging tenants fees for repairs the landlord must make anyway | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB121 | Required rent to be advertised and listed as one all-in figure and set payment-method rules | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 AB298 | Required refunds of rental application fees in some cases and a plain-language appendix of every fee in a lease | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2025 AB280 | Required refunds of rental application fees in some cases and a plain-language appendix of every fee in a lease | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2023 AB218 | Required rent to be advertised and listed as one all-in figure and set payment-method rules | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2023 SB78 | Regulated security deposits and the fees landlords charge prospective tenants, and how unpaid rent is collected | Vetoed | Vetoed by the governor |
| 2019 SB256 | Capped late-rent fees and tightened security-deposit return rules for renters | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2021 SB218 | Regulated fees landlords charge prospective tenants and rental application practices | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2021 AB332 | Proposed a statewide registry of landlords reporting units owned and rents charged | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2025 SB436 | Created a statewide registry of larger landlords with a tenant complaint mechanism | Failed | Died in its first committee |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- AB121 passed in 2025 where the nearly identical AB218 was vetoed in 2023 - what changed in the text, and does that map a passable version of the application-fee bill?
- Who enforces the existing fee rules today, and with what budget?

## 7. Encourage ADUs, smaller lots, and higher density / infill - 'nobody has done anything legislatively.'

- **Status:** Recently done, in part
- **The record:** 'Nobody has done anything legislatively' was accurate through 2023 and stopped being accurate in 2025. 2025 AB396 requires every large city and county (Clark, Washoe, and the five big cities) to adopt an ordinance allowing accessory dwelling units on residential lots - passed 27-15 and 14-7, now law. 2025 AB241 requires every city and county to allow multifamily and mixed-use housing by right on commercially zoned land - passed 28-14 and 15-6, now law. The older precedent is 2021 SB150 (Sen. Dallas Harris, with cross-party sponsors), requiring big jurisdictions to allow tiny houses in some zoning districts - 20-1 and 33-8. What keeps failing is the tax side: the property-tax exemption for ADUs rented to voucher tenants died in 2023 (AB416) and again in 2025 (AB131) - the 2025 version passed the Assembly 42-0 and still died in the Senate.
- **Where the decision sits:** Participants said this is 'largely a county code issue' - the 2025 laws work exactly that way: the state orders local ordinances into existence, and lot-level rules remain local.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 AB396 | Required big cities and counties to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB241 | Required every city and county to allow apartments and mixed-use housing by right on commercial land | Enacted | Became law |
| 2021 SB150 | Required big cities and counties to allow tiny houses in some zoning districts | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 AB416 | Property-tax break for accessory dwelling units rented to housing-voucher tenants | Failed | Died on its first floor / calendar |
| 2025 AB131 | Property-tax break for accessory dwelling units rented to housing-voucher tenants | Failed | Died in the second house |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- AB396/AB241 are new: what do the local implementing ordinances actually allow, and are permits being issued?
- Is the missing piece now financing (the twice-dead ADU tax exemption) rather than legality?

## 8. Incentivize builders to build starter/tiny/modular homes - not mobile homes.

- **Status:** Tried once; stalled early
- **The record:** The exact design - incentives for builders of entry-priced houses - was filed once: 2025 SB430 offered transferable tax credits and tax abatements for projects building single-family homes sold at cost to buyers under 130 percent of area median income, with resale protections keeping the homes out of corporate-investor hands. It died in Senate Revenue and Economic Development without a vote. The adjacent precedents are legal-status bills, not incentives: 2021 SB150 made big jurisdictions allow tiny houses in some zones; 2025 AB38 modernized the whole manufactured/factory-built housing code (40-2 and 17-3). Participants' skepticism that incentives can beat market pricing has never actually been tested in Nevada law.
- **Where the decision sits:** State lever for tax incentives and building-code status; land costs and builder economics - the constraint participants emphasized - sit outside any bill in this record.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 SB430 | Tax credits and abatements for builders of starter homes sold at cost to moderate-income buyers, kept out of corporate-investor hands | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 SB150 | Required big cities and counties to allow tiny houses in some zoning districts | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB38 | Omnibus update of manufactured, mobile and factory-built housing regulation | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- SB430 died with no vote in a committee its own chair sponsored it into - fiscal note or design?
- Does the AB38 factory-built framework plus SB150 zoning cover the 'different materials, modular building' idea without new incentives?

## 9. Student-loan forgiveness / targeted homebuying subsidies for high-demand professions (teachers, firefighters, nurses) - borrow the rural-medicine model.

- **Status:** Never filed
- **The record:** No bill in this record ties student-loan forgiveness or a homebuying subsidy to service in a profession. The model participants cited is real and growing on the health side - the Student Loan Repayment for Providers of Health Care in Underserved Communities program exists in statute and was expanded twice in 2025 (AB269, SB266, both enacted) - but no one has extended the design to housing or to teachers and firefighters. The closest housing attempts: 2023 SB47 let school districts build, buy, or rent homes for their own employees and died on the Senate floor; 2025 SB193's mortgage-rate buy-down pilot named eligible borrowers by income, not profession, and died in the Assembly.
- **Where the decision sits:** State lever (the health precedent is a state program); counties defining their own priority professions, as participants proposed, would need state authorization for any state-funded version.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 SB47 | Let school districts build, buy or rent homes for teachers and other employees | Failed | Died on its first floor / calendar |
| 2025 SB193 | State pilot to buy down mortgage interest rates for eligible first-home borrowers | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2025 AB269 | Expanded the student-loan repayment program for health care providers in underserved areas; a health-workforce bill | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 SB266 | Added behavioral health providers to the student-loan repayment program for underserved areas; a health-workforce bill | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Why has a design with an enacted in-state template never been filed for housing?
- Would it route through the education committees (like SB47) or the housing/commerce committees (like SB193)?

## 10. Pass legislation convening a working group of builders, citizens, and legislators to fix housing together.

- **Status:** Precedent exists
- **The record:** The machinery exists. 2019 AB476 (cross-party sponsors including Benitez-Thompson and Republican co-sponsors) recreated the statutory Advisory Committee on Housing inside the Housing Division, revised its membership, powers and duties, required an annual statewide housing-needs report, and gave the Committee the right to request one bill draft per session - passed 38-2 and 20-0. Local versions also passed: 2019 AB73 created a Clark County working group on homelessness; 2019 AB240 made five northern counties report jointly on growth. Study bills in general are the most reliably enacted kind of housing bill in this record (4 of 5 housing-studies bills passed; 2025 AB457's corporate-landlord tax study is the newest). The failure is narrow: 2025 AB37, which upgraded the Housing Division's Housing Advocate into a Housing Liaison with new duties, passed the Assembly 42-0 and died in the Senate at the deadline.
- **Where the decision sits:** Fully a state-legislature lever, and the cheapest one in the record. The open design question is composition: nothing in the collected digests shows guaranteed seats for builders and ordinary citizens alongside legislators, which is the specific three-legged table participants described.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 AB476 | Recreated the state Advisory Committee on Housing to advise the Housing Division | Enacted | Became law |
| 2019 AB73 | Created a temporary Clark County working group on reducing homelessness and its funding | Enacted | Became law |
| 2019 AB240 | Made five northern counties report jointly on managing growth in the region | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB37 | Turned the Housing Advocate into a Housing Liaison and updated the Housing Division's duties | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2025 AB457 | Ordered an interim study of taxing corporate rental-property owners as single entities and taxing controlling-interest transfers | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Does the existing Advisory Committee on Housing already have builder and citizen seats, and does it meet?
- Would a working group produce different output than the Committee's existing annual report plus its one bill draft per session?
