Glossary — Housing Affordability Brief

Plain-language definitions for the terms, ideas, and acronyms used in “Housing Affordability in Nevada” (citizen brief v3.0).

How Nevada lawmaking works

Process terms that appear throughout the brief.

Session / special session. The Nevada Legislature meets in a regular session of 120 days in odd-numbered years — 2019, 2021, 2023, and 2025 in this record. A bill that has not finished every step when the session ends is dead and must start over in a future session. A special session is a short extra meeting called for specific topics, like the November 2025 session where the corporate-purchase cap missed by one vote.

Chambers. The Legislature’s two houses: the Assembly (42 members) and the Senate (21 members). A bill must pass both chambers to reach the governor.

Vote pairs such as “14–6 and 28–14.” The brief lists floor votes as yes–no tallies, one per chamber. 42–0 is a unanimous Assembly vote; 21–0 is a unanimous Senate vote.

Committee / first committee. A small panel of lawmakers that studies a bill and votes on whether to send it onward. Every bill starts in a committee, and most bills that fail die there — in their “first committee” — without the full chamber ever voting.

Floor vote / floor calendar. A vote of the full chamber, and the schedule of bills waiting for one. A bill can die “at the deadline” when time runs out before its turn.

Two-thirds majority. Nevada’s constitution requires a two-thirds vote in each chamber — 28 of 42 in the Assembly, 14 of 21 in the Senate — for any measure that raises revenue, including fees. SB10’s investor-registry fees triggered this rule, which is how a 27–10 win still fell one vote short.

Veto. The governor’s rejection of a bill that passed both chambers. A vetoed bill does not become law unless two-thirds of each chamber votes to override.

Delivered / no signature, no chapter. After passing both chambers, a bill is “delivered” to the governor. A signed bill is assigned a chapter number in state law; “no signature, no chapter” means the official record simply ends — the bill never became law.

Sponsor. The lawmaker or committee that formally introduces a bill. Cross-party teams are bills with named sponsors from both parties.

Interim study. Work lawmakers order between sessions to examine a topic and report back — like the corporate rental-owner taxation study due for 2027.

Statute. A law on the books. “In statute” means an idea is already law.

Housing and development terms

Subject terms used in the brief’s proposal sections.

Accessory dwelling unit (ADU). A small second home on the same lot as a main house — a casita, garage apartment, or backyard cottage. AB396 (2025) requires the largest jurisdictions to allow them.

By right. Development a property owner may build without asking for special permission — no rezoning, no discretionary vote. AB241 (2025) makes apartments and mixed-use housing by right on commercial land.

Inclusionary requirement. A mandate that new developments include a percentage of affordable units — never filed in Nevada.

In-lieu fund / in-lieu fee. Letting a developer pay money into a housing fund instead of building required affordable units.

Linkage fee. A charge on new residential development, dedicated to affordable housing (SB99, 2025).

Impact fee. A charge on new development to fund the infrastructure it will need — roads, sewers, schools (SB471’s school version, 2019).

Corporate-purchase cap. An annual limit on how many homes corporations and LLCs may buy — the design of SB395, SB391, and SB10.

Investor registry. A required state register of investor-owned rental homes, paired with the cap in SB10.

Rent cap / rent control. A legal limit on how much rent can rise; “the cap-ban” refers to the mirror-image bills that would forbid cities from adopting any rent control.

Manufactured-home park. A community of factory-built homes whose owners typically rent the lot under them — why SB275’s rent cap targeted parks and the seniors living in them.

All-in pricing. Requiring the advertised rent to be the real total, with mandatory fees included and disclosed (AB121, 2025).

Application fee / late fee / grace period. The fee to apply for a rental; the penalty for late rent (capped at 5 percent since 2019); and the three-day window after the due date before a late fee may be charged (since 2021).

Landlord registry. A proposed statewide list of rental-property owners; died in first committee in 2021 and 2025.

Eviction diversion. Programs that resolve nonpayment cases — through mediation or rental assistance — before they become evictions (AB475, 2025).

Voucher holders. Renters who pay part of their rent with a federal housing voucher (often called Section 8); the twice-failed ADU tax break rewarded landlords who rent to them.

Matched-savings accounts. Savings accounts where donors match what a family deposits toward a first home (SB188, 2021).

Rate buy-down. Paying money up front to lower a mortgage’s interest rate; SB193 (2025) would have piloted state buy-downs for first-home borrowers.

Tiny house / factory-built housing. Very small homes, and homes built in factories and assembled on site; SB150 (2021) legalized tiny houses in some zones and AB38 (2025) modernized the factory-built code.

At-cost starter homes / resale protections. Homes sold at construction cost to moderate-income buyers, with rules keeping them from being flipped to investors — SB430’s untested design.

Attainable housing. Housing affordable to working households; the Nevada Attainable Housing Account (AB540, 2025) is the state’s $133 million fund for it.

Housing Division / bond authority. The state housing agency, which finances homebuyer programs by issuing bonds rather than through session-by-session laws.

Housing Advocate / Housing Liaison. The Housing Division’s public-facing ombudsman role; the 2025 bill upgrading the Advocate into a Liaison passed the Assembly 42–0 and died in the Senate.

Advisory Committee on Housing. The statutory builders-and-citizens advisory body recreated by AB476 (2019), with an annual statewide needs report and the right to request one bill draft per session.

Windsor Park relocation act. The state law relocating residents of Windsor Park, a North Las Vegas neighborhood where sinking ground has damaged homes; revised by SB6 of the 2025 special session.

Hedge fund. An investment fund that pools money from large investors — shorthand in the corporate-ownership debate for institutional buyers of single-family homes.

Federal programs and terms

Terms from the brief’s federal-overlap section.

Low-Income Housing Tax Credit. The main federal subsidy for building affordable rentals — tax credits investors buy, financing the projects.

HUD block grants. Flexible federal housing and community-development money sent to states and cities by the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

FHA / VA loans. Federally backed mortgages — insured by the Federal Housing Administration or guaranteed for veterans by the Department of Veterans Affairs — that let buyers qualify with smaller down payments.

Junk-fee rule. The 2024 federal rule requiring up-front disclosure of mandatory fees — it covers lodging and event tickets, not rental housing.

Acronyms and abbreviations

Every shortened form used in the brief.

AB / SB. Assembly Bill / Senate Bill — the chamber a bill started in, plus its number. Numbering restarts every session, so a number can repeat across years.

D / R. Democrat / Republican, shown after a legislator’s name.

ADU. Accessory dwelling unit.

LLC. Limited liability company — the business structure many investor landlords use.

HUD. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development.

FHA. Federal Housing Administration.

VA. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs.
