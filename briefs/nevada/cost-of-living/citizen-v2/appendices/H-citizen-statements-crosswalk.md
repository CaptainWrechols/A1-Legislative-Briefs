# Appendix H — Citizen statements, checked against the record

The ten most common citizen statements on the rising cost of living (healthcare focus) from the Phase 2 Community Conversations (RAG constituent-voice dataset), each checked against the 2019–2025 bill record. "Never filed" means no bill in this record proposed it — not that it is impossible.


## 1. Adopt interstate licensure compacts (e.g., the Nurse Licensure Compact) to expand provider supply.

- **Status:** Tried; passed for some professions, stalled for nurses
- **The record:** Compacts pass easily for some professions and keep dying for nurses. Enacted, all with unanimous or near-unanimous floor votes: the Physical Therapy Compact (2019 SB186; re-enacted 2025 AB248, 41-1 and 21-0), the EMS personnel compact (2023 AB158, 42-0 and 21-0), the Audiology and Speech-Language Pathology Compact (2025 AB230, 41-0 and 21-0), and licensure by endorsement for dental hygienists (2025 AB334, 42-0 and 21-0). The Nurse Licensure Compact - the compact citizens named - has been filed three times and never reached a floor vote: 2021 AB142 (a Republican sponsor team) and 2023 AB108 both died in Assembly Commerce and Labor, and in 2025 it was folded into SB34, a five-compact omnibus (PA, nurse, audiology/SLP, PT, OT) that died in Senate Commerce and Labor while the audiology/SLP and PT compacts passed as separate bills. For physicians, the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact already sits in Nevada statute from before this record's window - SB34's own digest places the PA compact 'in the same chapter as the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact' - and the record's physician measures ease individual licensure further: 2025 SB124 (foreign medical graduates' path to full licensure) passed 41-0 and 21-0, while the 2019 provisional-license design (SB259) died on the Senate floor.
- **Where the decision sits:** Fully a state-legislature lever: compacts are enacted as state statutes. The 25-year multi-state findings participants cited are not in this dataset; what the Nevada record shows is the pattern of which compacts reached a floor.

- What objection stops the nurse compact in committee while the PT, EMS, and audiology compacts pass unanimously - and is it different in Assembly versus Senate Commerce and Labor?

- The physician compact is already in Nevada statute - is the licensure barrier participants described a compact gap at all, or a board-processing one?

## 2. Increase the overall supply of physicians and providers.

- **Status:** Tried; finished in the special session
- **The record:** The largest supply bill in the record became law three months late. 2025 SB434 (Senate Majority Leader Cannizzaro) created the Statewide Health Care Access and Recruitment Grant Program - competitive grants against critical provider shortages - passed the Senate 18-2 and the Assembly 42-0 as amended, and died on the session's last day when the Senate did not concur in the Assembly amendment. The 36th (2025) Special Session then enacted the same program as SB5 (15-6 and 37-0, Chapter 12), housing it under the new Nevada Health Authority. Around it sits a steady record: Health Service Corps funding enacted in 2019 (SB289) and 2021 (SB233), dental therapy licensed in 2019 (SB366), workforce-data laws enacted in 2021 (AB278, SB379). The failures cluster at the money stage: the 2023 Health Service Corps refill (AB248) died in committee, the workforce-needs assessment (2025 SB495) passed the Senate 13-8 and died in the Assembly, and a proposed health-workforce office (2025 SB425) died on the Senate floor.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state-legislature lever, and now an existing program: the Statewide Health Care Access and Recruitment Grant Program is in statute with an account under the Nevada Health Authority. The 'roughly 500 doctors' gap participants cited is not measured in this dataset.

## 3. Fund more graduate medical education / residency programs.

- **Status:** Precedent exists
- **The record:** A state residency-grant program is already in statute: 2023 SB350 created grants for new and expanded physician residency programs (passed 21-0 and 40-0) and 2025 SB262 revised it (21-0 and 42-0) - both carried by Sen. Julie Pazina (D) with co-sponsors from both parties' orbit. What keeps failing is the funding design that runs through tax credits: Sen. Robin Titus's (R) bills giving businesses credits for donations to residency programs died on the Senate floor in 2023 (SB369) and 2025 (SB269), and the 2019 direct appropriation for postgraduate residencies (AB311, Krasner R) died in its first committee. The pathway bills pass: 2025 SB124 (Doñate D) eased foreign medical graduates' path to full licensure, 41-0 and 21-0. The combined debt-plus-residency design (2023 AB393, Kasama R + Lange D) died in its first committee, and 2025 AB170's associate-physician licenses died the same way.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state-legislature lever with a program already on the books; residency slots also depend on federal Medicare GME funding, which no Nevada bill controls - the state bills fund state grants on top.

## 4. Offer student-loan / medical-debt forgiveness for providers serving in-state or in rural areas.

- **Status:** Already in statute and expanding
- **The record:** Participants described this as not yet drafted; the record shows it is Nevada's most consistently enacted recruitment tool. The Nevada Health Service Corps - loan repayment for providers who commit to underserved areas - was funded in 2019 (SB289, 41-0 and 21-0) and 2021 (SB233, 42-0 and 19-0), both carried by Sen. Joseph Hardy (R). 2023 AB45 created a broader program repaying student loans of health providers working in underserved communities (41-0 and 21-0). In 2025 the Legislature expanded eligibility twice: AB269 added speech-language pathologists, physical therapists and others (40-2 and 21-0), and SB266 widened qualifying provider types again (42-0 and 21-0). The failures are the bigger-money versions: the 2019 stand-alone loan-repayment program (AB358, Republican sponsors) died on the origin floor, the 2021 physician-stipend design (AB372, Kasama R) died in committee, the 2023 Health Service Corps refill (AB248) died in committee, and the behavioral-health expansion (AB69) died on the origin floor in 2023.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state-legislature lever with the machinery already in statute; the rural-funding tie-in participants floated (federal rural money as the source) does not appear in any bill in this record.

## 5. Reform / limit insurance prior authorization (including AI-driven denials).

- **Status:** Tried; partially done in 2025
- **The record:** 2025 was the breakthrough-and-veto year. What passed: AB463 (Backus D) tightened prior-authorization rules and deadlines for private insurers, Medicaid, and the Children's Health Insurance Program - 42-0 and 20-0, now law. What was vetoed: SB128 (Neal D), barring insurers from relying solely on artificial intelligence to deny prior-authorization requests - the AI-denial concern participants raised - passed the Assembly 23-16 and the Senate 15-6 and was vetoed. AB290 (Nguyen D), the bill participants named as having died and being planned for reintroduction, cleared Assembly Commerce and Labor with amendments, was re-referred, and died without further action at session's end; three other 2025 prior-auth bills (AB295, AB470, SB398 - two of them Republican-led) died in their first committees. The earlier record: all three 2019 attempts died in committee (AB225, AB372, SB359), while the adjacent step-therapy limits passed unanimously in 2023 (SB167, SB194) and dental-denial review by a dentist passed in 2025 (AB202, 42-0 and 20-0).
- **Where the decision sits:** State law reaches state-regulated plans, Medicaid, and CHIP - AB463 covers exactly that set. The record does not show what the SB128 veto objected to (no veto messages in this dataset).

## 6. Reduce administrative burden and eliminate insurance middlemen, including pharmacy benefit managers.

- **Status:** Tried repeatedly; stalled late
- **The record:** The transparency layer is law; the regulation layer keeps dying within sight of the finish. Enacted: 2019 AB141 (Republican-carried) barred PBMs from gagging pharmacists who tell patients about cheaper options; 2019 SB276 ordered the drug-cost and rebate study; 2019 SB378 made Medicaid drug contractors pass rebates back to the state; 2023 AB434 protected 340B providers from PBM penalties; and 2025 SB389 (Scheible D + Stone R) put Medicaid prescription drugs under a single state-contracted PBM, 41-0 and 18-2. The failures escalate in reach and die later: licensing PBMs under the state health department died in committee in 2021 (SB392); making PBMs follow the same rules as the insurers they serve passed the Senate 21-0 in 2023 (SB352, Scheible D + Buck R) and died in the Assembly; the 2025 rewrite SB316 (Nguyen D + Stone R + Titus R) passed the Senate 18-2 and the Assembly 42-0 and died on the final day without concurrence; and the two committee-stage retries (SB149, SB209) died in 2025. No bill in this record proposes eliminating middlemen outright - every design regulates, licenses, or consolidates them.
- **Where the decision sits:** State law reaches PBMs serving state-regulated plans and Medicaid; the enacted 2025 design consolidates Medicaid pharmacy middlemen into one audited contract rather than removing the function.

## 7. Improve provider-network accuracy and speed up insurance credentialing.

- **Status:** Barely tried
- **The record:** This is the thinnest record of the ten proposals. What exists: 2019 SB234 requires insurers to use a standardized letter and report trends when they reject a provider's application to join a network - passed 21-0 and 39-0, the one direct network-admission law in the set. 2023 SB494 funded Medicaid's computer systems including centralized provider credentialing (enacted, 20-0 and 42-0) - an administrative modernization, not a mandate on insurers. No bill in this record sets credentialing deadlines for insurers, audits network-directory accuracy, or defines 'full' panels. The adjacent attempts died: 2019 SB290 (Hardy R, network plans paying out-of-network providers for unanticipated care) died in its first committee, and 2021 SB90 (Hardy R, keeping unfounded investigations from counting against providers in credentialing) passed the Senate 21-0 and died in the Assembly.
- **Where the decision sits:** Split lever: state law can regulate credentialing of state-licensed plans (SB234 shows the form); network contracts and panel-size decisions are otherwise private contracting, and Medicaid credentialing runs through the state's own systems (SB494).

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 SB234 | Made insurers use a standard letter and report trends when rejecting doctors from their provider networks. | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 SB494 | Funds Medicaid agency computer systems, including utilization review and centralized provider credentialing. | Enacted | Became law |
| 2019 SB290 | Made insurer network plans pay out-of-network providers for unanticipated care, addressing surprise bills. | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 SB90 | Relabeled unfounded investigations of health providers so they do not count against licensure, credentialing or insurance. | Failed | Died in the second house |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- A credentialing-deadline or directory-accuracy mandate has never been filed - what would its committee path be, Commerce and Labor or Health and Human Services?
- Did SB234's rejection-letter and trend-reporting data ever get analyzed, and does it show the 'full panel' pattern participants described?

## 8. Expand insurance pooling for small businesses and non-employer groups.

- **Status:** Precedent exists
- **The record:** Nevada has legislated in this space in every direction except the one participants sketched. 2019 SB481 set state rules for association health plans and self-funded multiple-employer arrangements offering small-business coverage (passed 21-0 and 39-0). 2021 SB396 let public agencies pool with private entities to purchase prescription drugs and medical supplies (21-0 and 40-1). The biggest structure is the Public Option: 2021 SB420 (Cannizzaro D) created a state-designed plan any Nevadan can buy - the record's rare contested enactment, 12-9 in the Senate and 26-15 in the Assembly. A 2019 study resolution (SCR10) on a public health-coverage plan was adopted. What died: the 2019 state bulk drug-purchasing list (SB226, Spearman D) in its first committee. No bill in this record creates an any-group pooling mechanism - the association/credit-union design participants floated has never been filed.
- **Where the decision sits:** Partly a state lever: the record's bills regulate state-licensed association plans and state purchasing. The employer-based structure participants called the key obstacle is federal tax and benefits law; no state bill in this record can or does repeal it.

- **Status:** Tried; narrow raises pass, broad reform stalls
- **The record:** The record splits cleanly by scope. Targeted Medicaid raises become law, usually unanimously: autism behavior-technician rates to at least $52/hour (2021 SB96), letting provider-assessment money fund higher Medicaid payments to more provider types (2023 SB435, after the Assembly version AB197 died on the origin floor), special billing categories and rates for children's cancer clinics (2023 SB221) and mental-health training clinics (2025 SB353), family-caregiver payment (2025 SB185, 41-1 and 21-0), epilepsy-device rates (2025 AB284), and dropping the out-of-state cap on autism rates (2025 SB497). The backdrop: the 31st (2020) Special Session's budget bill AB3 cut FY2020-2021 appropriations including Department of Health and Human Services budgets (passed 36-6 and 21-0).

## 10. Right-size the care setting (urgent care vs. ER) and use rotating/staffed clinics for access.

- **Status:** No bill on the specific model
- **The record:** The split-billing model participants designed - separating ER from urgent care so minor issues don't incur ER charges, with rotating physicians or a state-paid physician per county - has never been filed in this record. What exists around its edges: the 2019 out-of-network emergency billing law (AB469, 38-3 and 21-0) limits what out-of-network providers can bill patients for emergency care; 2023 SB497 adjusted the arbitration system for those payment disputes; 2019 AB317 made off-campus hospital sites offering urgent care or emergency care carry distinct provider identifiers; and 2023 AB277 (Koenig R) let small 24/7 rural emergency hospitals operate in Nevada, 41-0 and 21-0. The bills that tried to control what emergency facilities charge died early: 2023 AB85 (Orentlicher D, a state process to set hospital and emergency-center prices) died on the origin floor, and 2025 AB349 (Orentlicher D, capping charges for public-employee plans at Medicare multiples) died in its first committee. Telehealth - the other access lever participants debated - is largely settled law: 2021 SB5 modernized coverage, 2023 SB119 kept payment parity, and 2023 AB276 extended it to provider-to-provider consultation.
- **Where the decision sits:** Mixed lever: Nevada licenses facility types (AB277 shows the form) and regulates billing of state-regulated plans, but ER billing practice also runs through federal Medicare rules - 2019 AB232 (requiring most hospitals to join Medicare, obligating emergency care regardless of ability to pay) is where the record touches that layer. County-level staffed clinics would be a county budget question no bill in this record addresses.

| 2023 SB497 | Adjusts arbitration rules for payment disputes over out-of-network emergency care. | Enacted | Became law |
| 2019 AB317 | Required off-campus hospital sites offering surgery, urgent or emergency care to use separate provider identifiers. | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 AB277 | Allowed rural emergency hospitals, small 24/7 emergency facilities, to operate in Nevada. | Enacted | Became law |
