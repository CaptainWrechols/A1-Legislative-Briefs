Glossary — K-12 Outcomes Brief

Plain-language definitions for the terms, ideas, and acronyms used in “K-12 Educational Outcomes in Nevada” (citizen brief v3.0).

How Nevada lawmaking works

Process terms that appear throughout the brief.

Session / special session. The Nevada Legislature meets in a regular session of 120 days in odd-numbered years — 2019, 2021, 2023, and 2025 in this record. A bill that has not finished every step when the session ends is dead and must start over in a future session. A special session is a short extra meeting called for specific topics, like the 2020 session that cut budgets during the pandemic.

Chambers. The Legislature’s two houses: the Assembly (42 members) and the Senate (21 members). A bill must pass both chambers to reach the governor.

Vote pairs such as “40–0 and 21–0.” The brief lists floor votes as yes–no tallies, one per chamber. 42–0 is a unanimous Assembly vote; 21–0 is a unanimous Senate vote.

Committee / first committee. A small panel of lawmakers that studies a bill and votes on whether to send it onward. Every bill starts in a committee, and most bills that fail die there — in their “first committee” — without the full chamber ever voting.

Money committees. Assembly Ways and Means and Senate Finance — the committees that review anything costing money. Bills with price tags routinely stop there without a vote against the policy itself.

Floor vote. A vote of the full chamber.

Second house. After passing the chamber where it started, a bill repeats the whole process — committee, then floor — in the other chamber.

Concurrence. When one chamber amends a bill, the other must vote to accept — “concur in” — the change before the bill can go to the governor. AB386 (2025) died awaiting exactly that final vote.

Veto. The governor’s rejection of a bill that passed both chambers. A vetoed bill does not become law unless two-thirds of each chamber votes to override.

Sponsor. The lawmaker or committee that formally introduces a bill. Cross-party sponsorship means named sponsors from both parties — more common in education than in any other subject The Forum has mapped.

Biennium funding act. The education budget bill covering the two years between sessions (SB503 in 2023, SB500 in 2025).

Statutory. Written into state law — a “statutory home” is a permanent place in statute for a function or board.

Education and school-system terms

Subject terms used in the brief’s proposal sections.

Pupil-Centered Funding Plan / Nevada Plan. Nevada’s school funding formula since 2019 (SB543), which attaches dollars to each pupil with weights for need — replacing the 1967-era Nevada Plan.

Commission on School Funding. The statutory body charged with calculating what fully funding Nevada schools would take.

Mining tax routed to education. The 2021 tax on gold and silver mining (AB495) whose proceeds go to schools.

Read by Grade 3. Nevada’s early-literacy program: pupils are assessed through third grade, and those reading far behind get mandatory intensive help. SB278 (2025) made the statewide assessment its sole trigger.

Retention. Holding a pupil back to repeat a grade. Automatic third-grade retention was removed in 2019 (AB289) and no bill has proposed restoring it.

Statewide assessment. The standardized test every Nevada pupil takes; the anchor for school ratings and the Read by Grade 3 trigger.

School star ratings. Nevada’s one-to-five-star report card for each school, built from test results and other measures.

Growth vs. proficiency. Two ways to judge schools: how much pupils improve over a year (growth) versus how many clear a fixed bar (proficiency). Nevada’s ratings already include a growth component; SB351 would have doubled its weight for the lowest-scoring pupils.

Competency-based education. Letting pupils advance by demonstrating mastery rather than by hours spent in a seat (SB403, 2025).

Social-emotional measurement. Tracking skills like self-management and relationships alongside academics (SB313, SB314).

End-of-course finals. The statewide exams once required to finish certain high-school courses; ended in 2023 (SB9).

Charter authority. The State Public Charter School Authority, which oversees charter schools and is covered by AB517’s recurring audits.

Inspector General (IG). An independent watchdog office with power to investigate an agency from outside — the never-heard design for auditing school spending.

Six-year outside-consultant reviews. The financial reviews of districts by outside consultants that existing law already requires every six years.

Public Education Oversight Board. The new statewide accountability board created by SB460 (2025).

Appointed nonvoting trustees. School-board members appointed rather than elected, seated without a vote — AB175 (2023) added four to the Clark County board; the 2025 bill giving them full voting rights and term limits died in Senate Education.

Superintendent. A district’s chief executive, hired by the elected school board — which is why the $485,000 contracts have never been touched by a bill.

Collective bargaining. Contract negotiation between a district and its employee unions; AB155 (2025), adding class size to bargaining, was vetoed.

Hard-to-fill positions. Teaching jobs districts struggle to staff — special education, rural posts — now eligible for extra pay (AB398, 2025).

Provisional hiring. Letting a licensed out-of-state teacher start work for a year while Nevada licensure paperwork finishes (AB49, 2025).

Interstate Teacher Mobility Compact. The multi-state agreement honoring other states’ teacher licenses (SB442, 2023).

Teaching-pathway grants. Paid routes into the profession for aspiring teachers (AB515, AB428, 2023).

Three-cueing. A reading-instruction method that has pupils guess words from context and pictures rather than sounding them out; the 2023 ban (AB187) died in committee.

Cross-zone transfers. Letting a pupil attend a school outside the attendance zone assigned to their address, with transportation grants (AB533, 2025).

“Where money allows” mandates. Staffing requirements — such as school social workers — that apply only to the extent funding exists. The unconditional versions die in committee; the money-permitting versions pass.

Ed-tech commission. The state’s dedicated educational-technology commission, abolished in 2023 (SB214).

Screen time. Pupils’ time on classroom devices; no bill limits it in the early grades.

Federal programs and terms

Terms from the brief’s federal-overlap section.

Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA). The federal education law requiring annual statewide testing in grades 3–8 and once in high school. Dropping Nevada’s statewide test would collide with it — but it allows growth-based accountability.

Federal single audit. The annual independent audit required of any district spending significant federal money.

FERPA / COPPA. The federal laws protecting pupil records (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act) and children’s online data (Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act) — the ground Nevada’s pupil-data disclosure law built on.

Head Start. The federal pre-kindergarten program for low-income families. Universal federal pre-K has been proposed but never enacted.

Federal teacher loan forgiveness. Existing federal programs that cancel student debt for teachers who serve qualifying schools.

Acronyms and abbreviations

Every shortened form used in the brief.

AB / SB. Assembly Bill / Senate Bill — the chamber a bill started in, plus its number. Numbering restarts every session, so a number can repeat across years.

D / R. Democrat / Republican, shown after a legislator’s name.

K-12 / K-5. Kindergarten through grade 12 (all of public school) / kindergarten through grade 5 (elementary).

AI. Artificial intelligence — software that mimics human judgment; barred from school counselors’ duties by AB406 (2025).

IG. Inspector General.

PE. Physical education.

ESSA. Every Student Succeeds Act (federal).

FERPA. Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (federal).

COPPA. Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (federal).
