# K-12 Educational Outcomes in Nevada

The legislative record behind Nevadans' ten most common K-12 education proposals, 2019–2025 — organized by how close each idea has come to becoming law.

## What this brief covers

Education is the Legislature's biggest and most cross-party subject: 513 bills across the 2019–2025 sessions, 228 of them now law. The big funding fight settled early — SB543 (2019) replaced the old Nevada Plan with the Pupil-Centered Funding Plan — and every session since built on it. Raises, audits, funding acts, and licensing bills pass, usually near-unanimously, and get signed; what stalls is narrower — new oversight bodies, classroom-time rules, and changes to what gets measured — several came within one vote of becoming law.

- **228** became law
- **55** policy bills with sponsors from both parties
- **7** outside-audit bills — none got a vote

## Closest to law: bills that passed both chambers

*These bills passed both houses; only a veto or the end-of-session deadline stopped them.*

### Passed both chambers, but time ran out

**An alternative Read by Grade 3 test — AB386 (2025).** Let Washoe County pilot an alternative literacy test; it passed 40–0 and 21–0 as amended and died awaiting one final procedural vote at session's end — unanimous in both chambers, the record's most advanced unfinished bill.

### Vetoed at the governor's desk

All 13 education vetoes fall in 2023 and 2025 and hit specific kinds of bills: **the statewide children's mental-health consortium (AB265, 2023)**, vetoed after passing 42–0 and 20–0; **the substitute-teacher insurance subsidy (AB282, 2023)**, vetoed after 31–11 and 16–4; **class-size collective bargaining (AB155, 2025)**. Collective-bargaining, school-related health, and library bills are what stop at the governor's desk.

## Proven support: cleared a chamber or a committee

*Each bill won a committee or floor majority before stalling.*

**Measurement bills that won votes.** Competency-based education (SB403, 2025) passed the Senate 21–0 and died in the second chamber. The social-emotional measurement bills, SB313 (2023) and SB314 (2025), cleared committee both times — sponsored by Senators Roberta Lange (D) and Lisa Krasner (R), both still serving — and died waiting for a Senate floor vote; none was recorded against them.

**Full rights for appointed trustees — 2025.** Giving AB175's four appointed, nonvoting Clark County trustees full voting rights and term limits passed the Assembly 24–18 and died in Senate Education; the base law, AB175 (2023), passed 29–11 and 16–4 after appointed-seat bills died in committee three sessions running; the idea moves further each session.

## Already law — and the openings around it

*New law settles some ground — and shows where the Legislature keeps building.*

### New laws from the 2025 session

The 2025 session funded extra pay for hard-to-fill teaching jobs (AB398, 20–0 and 41–1), provisional out-of-state teacher hiring (AB49, 42–0 and 21–0), consequences for principals plus a Public Education Oversight Board (SB460, 21–0 and 38–4), a single test as the Read by Grade 3 trigger (SB278, 42–0 and 21–0), district cellphone policies (SB444, 42–0 and 21–0), an AI ban on counselor duties (AB406), school social workers where money allows (SB277, 38–1 and 15–5), $100 million in school-repair bonds (AB224, 41–1 and 21–0), cross-zone transfers (AB533), and the two-year budget act (SB500, 42–0 and 13–8). Bills repeating these are unlikely to advance.

### Where lawmakers appear willing to go further

Accountability laws keep expanding: AB517 (2023) ordered recurring audits of the two biggest districts and the charter authority, 42–0 and 21–0, first audits due 2026; SB460's oversight board gives future proposals a body to build on. Principal accountability is law twice over (SB292, 2023, 21–0 and 39–3; SB460). Nevada's ratings already include a growth measure — the failed bills only changed its weight — and testing has been cut twice (SB353, 2021, capping test time, 42–0 and 21–0; SB9, 2023, ending end-of-course finals). Teacher-recruitment bills keep passing near-unanimously: the Interstate Teacher Mobility Compact (SB442, 2023), paid teaching-pathway grants (AB515, AB428, 2023), provisional hiring (AB49), and raise money (SB231, 2023, 21–0 and 41–1). Pre-K investment is solid precedent — the grant program (SB84, 2019, 41–0 and 20–0), the early-literacy account (AB400, 2023), family engagement extended in 2025 (AB212, 38–4 and 21–0) — with seats added through the budget, not by law. The funding system itself was rebuilt in this record: SB543 (2019, 18–3 and 34–7), SB439 (2021), the education mining tax (AB495, 2021, 28–14 and 16–5), and the two-year budget acts (SB503, SB500); the Commission on School Funding calculates full funding. AB289 (2019) removed automatic third-grade retention, 28–11 and 17–4 — no bill since proposes reinstating it; when the 2020 special session cut the program's $31.4 million second-year transfer, it was listed among the first accounts to restore.

## Little traction yet: never filed or stopped early

*No votes yet — never filed, never heard, or stopped early; unproven rather than closed.*

**An outside audit of school spending.** Filed seven times, never a vote: AB146, AB296 (2019), AB108 (2021), AB149 (an Inspector General of Education), AB353 (2023), AB33, AB154 (2025), plus the oversight committee AB395 (2023). Assemblymember Brittney Miller (D), still serving, carried it four sessions; a Republican version met the same fate. What has passed keeps audits inside government: AB517's audits and the six-year outside reviews already in law — a 2025 repeal bill failed to remove them.

**Recess and classroom time.** AB53 (2025) — 20 minutes of daily outdoor recess in K-5 and a ban on withholding it — died in Assembly Education without a vote; a PE bill (SB182, 2021) and a fourth social-studies credit (AB228, 2023) also stalled. When leadership backs a course requirement, it passes nearly unanimously (financial literacy, AB274, 2023, 40–0 and 20–0; the 2025 cellphone rules).

**Growth-based ratings and funding guarantees.** SB351 (2025), double-weighting growth of the lowest-scoring pupils, died in Senate Education without a vote. AB459 (2023), tying the education budget to inflation plus enrollment, died in Ways and Means, as did its 2025 successor in Finance; SB58 (2025), widening pre-K grants, died in Senate Finance; the $45,000 minimum teacher salary (SB445, 2019, Republican-sponsored) and the grade-3 class-size cap (AB335, 2023) died in committee. Early-childhood offices failed on the Assembly floor in 2023 (AB113) and in committee in 2025 (SB82).

**Never filed at all.** No bill requires proof that classroom technology helps learning or limits early-grade screen time — the closest bill, SB248 (2025), died in Senate Education; the ed-tech commission was abolished in 2023 (SB214). No bill proposes universal pre-K or touches superintendent pay ($485,000 contracts are signed by elected boards). The three-cueing reading ban (AB187, 2023), counselor staffing with no funding limits (AB298, 2025), and monthly mental-health workshops (AB374, 2025) died in committee.

## Where there is movement — and where there is none


**Moving:** teacher pay and recruitment; administrator accountability; funding and facilities; trimming testing; and mental-health staffing laws (SB319, 2019, 41–0 and 21–0; SB151, 2021; SB277, 2025). **Not moving:** the outside-audit idea (seven bills, no vote yet); recess and instructional-time rules; superintendent pay (no bill ever); universal pre-K; ed-tech purchasing standards (never filed); and staffing rules without funding limits, which die in committee while “as money allows” versions pass.

**Where bills stop, and who sponsors them.** Education committees stop the most bills at the first stop (127), the money committees 40 — mostly bills with price tags — and Government Affairs 12, with most inspector-general bills. Cross-party sponsorship works: 28 of 55 two-party bills became law. Senator Scott Hammond (R, 31 bills) has left; Senators Marilyn Dondero Loop (D, 37), Carrie Buck (R, 31), Roberta Lange (D), and Lisa Krasner (R) and Assemblymembers Brittney Miller (D, audit bills) and Selena Torres-Fossett (D, teacher-recruitment bills) still serve.

## Federal overlap: what Washington already covers

*Where federal action covers or blocks a proposal — and where states are the only route.*

The federal Every Student Succeeds Act requires annual testing in grades 3–8 and once in high school — dropping Nevada's statewide test would break federal law — but ESSA allows growth-based ratings, so bills like SB351 are permitted. Districts spending federal money already get annual federal audits; proposed inspector-general audits would add to, not duplicate, them. Pupil-data privacy is federal ground (FERPA and COPPA, which SB403 of 2019 built on); no federal rule requires proof that classroom technology works. Head Start covers low-income pre-K; universal federal pre-K was proposed but never passed. There is no federal recess, class-size, or teacher-pay rule, though federal teacher loan-forgiveness exists.
