# Appendix H — Citizen statements, checked against the record

The ten most common citizen statements on K-12 educational outcomes from the Phase 2 Community Conversations (RAG constituent-voice dataset, "NV1 - Education" sheet), each checked against the 2019–2025 bill record. "Never filed" means no bill in this record proposed it — not that it is impossible.


## 1. Audit spending and direct more funding to the classroom

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** The independent inspector-general design has been filed seven times in four sessions and never reached a floor vote: 80:AB146 and 80:AB296 (2019), 81:AB108 (2021), 82:AB149 (an Inspector General of Education) and 82:AB353 (under the State Controller) in 2023, 83:AB33 and 83:AB154 (2025). A Financial Oversight of School Spending Committee (82:AB395) also died in its first committee. What passed is legislative auditing: 82:AB517 (2023) requires the Legislative Auditor to performance-audit the two biggest districts and the charter authority at least every four years - it passed 42-0 and 21-0. Where similar ideas stopped: First committee, every time: Government Affairs killed the statewide inspector-general bills (2019, 2021, 2025); the Education committees killed the education-specific versions (2023). 83:SB411 (2025), which would have repealed the existing six-year outside-consultant reviews of district financial management, also died in committee - the review requirement survives in statute (NRS 387.607).
- **Where the decision sits:** A state-legislature lever. Existing law already requires six-year financial-management reviews of districts by outside consultants and, since 2023, recurring legislative performance audits; the never-passed piece is an auditor independent of both the Legislature and the Education Department.

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Why did the same design die in two different committees under sponsors from both parties?

- Would an inspector general need new money, and does that route it through the money committees that killed 40 education bills?

## 2. Increase per-pupil funding / raise Nevada above the national average

- **Status:** Precedent exists; specific designs still stall
- **The record:** The machinery was rebuilt in this record: 80:SB543 (2019) created the State Education Fund and the Pupil-Centered Funding Plan (18-3, 34-7); 81:SB439 (2021) rebuilt its revenue streams (20-1, 36-5); 81:AB495 (2021) added a gold and silver mining excise tax routed to education (28-14, 16-5); 82:SB503 (2023) and 83:SB500 (2025) are the biennium funding acts (SB500 passed the Assembly 42-0 and the Senate 13-8). The 2023 session also passed 82:SB231, money for districts that budget raises for teachers and support staff. What keeps dying is the guarantee: 82:AB459 (2023), requiring the education budget to at least keep pace with revenue growth or inflation-plus-enrollment, died in Ways and Means; 83:SB471 (2025), changing how the Governor budgets the education baseline, died in Finance. Where similar ideas stopped: The money committees. Alternative revenue designs (county sales-tax options, 80:SB305; cannabis-tax rerouting, 83:AB307 and 83:AB508) died in Revenue, Ways and Means or on the floor.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state-legislature lever exercised every two years in the biennium budget. No bill in this record sets a national-average target in statute; the Commission on School Funding is the standing body that computes and recommends the gap.

## 3. Mandate/protect recess and guaranteed instructional minutes (PE, art, social studies)

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** The recess mandate reached the Legislature once: 83:AB53 (2025) would have required at least 20 minutes of daily outdoor recess in kindergarten through grade 5 and banned withholding recess for academic or behavioral reasons - exactly the enforcement gap participants described. It died in Assembly Education without a vote. 81:SB182 (2021), requiring PE to include activity scientifically shown to reduce obesity, died in Senate Education. 82:AB228 (2023), raising required high school social studies from three to four credits, cleared committee and died on the Assembly floor. Where similar ideas stopped: The first committee (Education), or the origin floor for the credit expansion. No minutes-guarantee bill has ever reached a floor vote.
- **Where the decision sits:** Split venue. Statute already prescribes required subjects (NRS 389.018) and 180 school days, so a minutes mandate is a state lever - but day-to-day recess practice currently lives in district wellness policies, which is why provision varies school to school.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 AB53 | Would have required at least 20 minutes of daily outdoor recess in elementary school and banned withholding it for academics or behavior | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 SB182 | Would have required PE to include activity scientifically shown to reduce obesity | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2023 AB228 | Would have raised required high school social studies from three to four credits including geography | Failed | Died on its first floor / calendar |
| 2023 AB274 | Required financial-literacy credits inside high school social studies and restructured the advisory council | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 SB444 | Required school cellphone policies with progressive discipline and instructional-time limits | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Why did AB53 never get a committee vote - opposition, or calendar?
- Would a grades-limited mandate (K-5 only, as AB53 was drafted) answer the age trade-off participants raised?
- Which districts already guarantee recess minutes by policy, making a statewide floor cheaper to enforce?

## 4. Adopt evidence-based, kid-centered classroom technology policies

- **Status:** Never filed
- **The record:** Nothing on the core design: no bill in this record requires independent proof of learning benefit before districts buy instructional technology, and no bill limits classroom screen time for young pupils. The nearest laws regulate the edges: 80:SB403 (2019) makes schools disclose which online services receive pupil data (40-0, 21-0); 81:SB66 (2021) tracks home Internet and device access; 83:SB444 (2025) requires district cellphone policies with instructional-time limits (42-0, 21-0); 83:AB406 (2025) bans artificial intelligence from doing school counselors' mental-health work (42-0, 20-1). The state's dedicated ed-tech body went the other way: 82:SB214 (2023) abolished the Commission on Educational Technology and folded its duties into the Department. Where similar ideas stopped: Nothing to stop - the proposal has not been filed. A 2025 transparency bill that would have required schools to post a public catalog of instructional materials including digital ones (83:SB248) died in Senate Education.
- **Where the decision sits:** Mostly a district procurement decision today. The state lever exists - the Legislature has shown it will regulate school technology (privacy, cellphones, AI) - but purchasing standards would be new statutory ground.

|---|---|---|---|

| 2021 SB66 | Made the state track pupils' home Internet and device access and work to close gaps | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 SB214 | Abolished the STEM council and educational technology commission, moving duties to the education department | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 SB444 | Required school cellphone policies with progressive discipline and instructional-time limits | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB406 | Banned artificial intelligence from doing counselors' mental-health work in schools | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 SB248 | Would have required every school to post a public catalog of library and instructional materials | Failed | Died in its first committee |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Should the standard bind the state's own grant money first (ed-tech bought with categorical funds) before binding district general funds?
- Who defines 'independent research' - the Department, or an outside body, given the Commission on Educational Technology no longer exists?
- Does the cellphone-policy law (83:SB444) open a template for device-time rules for younger grades?

## 5. Universal pre-K and early childhood investment

- **Status:** Precedent exists; specific designs still stall
- **The record:** No bill in this record proposes pre-K for all children. The investment machinery exists and keeps growing: 80:SB84 (2019) put the state pre-K grant program in statute (41-0, 20-0); 82:AB400 (2023) added an Early Childhood Literacy and Readiness Account (41-0, 20-1); 82:AB348 (2023) and 83:AB212 (2025) built and extended a virtual family-engagement program for children entering kindergarten (38-4, 21-0 in 2025). The structural bills keep dying: a statutory Office of Early Childhood Systems passed committee and died on the Assembly floor in 2023 (82:AB113), and its Senate successor died in committee in 2025 (83:SB82); 83:SB58 (2025), which would have widened pre-K grant eligibility and tied grant size to per-pupil funding, died in Senate Finance; 83:AB292 (2025), requiring new elementary buildings to include pre-K space, died in Assembly Education. Where similar ideas stopped: Money and structure: Finance for the eligibility expansion, the Assembly floor and Government Affairs for the office bills. Program money routed as grants passes nearly unanimously.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state lever exercised through the budget: pre-K seats grow through appropriations to the grant program, not through an entitlement statute. A universal guarantee would be new ground.

## 6. Strengthen accountability for administrators and superintendents

- **Status:** Precedent exists; specific designs still stall
- **The record:** Principal accountability is now law twice over: 82:SB292 (2023) makes principals at-will employees when their school's rating falls and half the teachers ask to leave (21-0, 39-3); 83:SB460 (2025) requires elementary achievement plans with consequences for principals and created a Public Education Oversight Board (21-0, 38-4). District-level accountability arrived the same way: 82:AB517's recurring performance audits. Superintendent pay has never been touched: no bill in this record caps it, links it to outcomes, or changes principal pay-by-enrollment. School-board restructuring keeps failing: appointed-seat bills died in committee in 2019 (80:AB57, 80:SB105, 80:AB491), 2021 (81:AB255, 81:SB111) and 2023 (82:SB64, 82:SB65) before 82:AB175 (2023) added four appointed nonvoting members to the Clark County board (29-11, 16-4); the 2025 follow-up giving them full rights and term limits (83:AB156) passed the Assembly 24-18 and died in Senate Education. Where similar ideas stopped: Education committees, session after session, for board restructuring; the Senate for the 2025 appointed-trustee upgrade. The enacted accountability bills were carried from the Senate leadership side.
- **Where the decision sits:** Split. Evaluation frameworks, ratings and audits are state levers and have moved. Superintendent contracts and principal pay schedules are district decisions made by elected boards under collective-bargaining law - the state record touches them only through the at-will law.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 SB292 | Made principals at-will when their school's rating falls and half the teachers seek transfers | Enacted | Became law |

| 2023 AB175 | Added four appointed nonvoting members to the Clark County school board | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB156 | Would have given Clark County's appointed trustees full rights, set term limits, and curbed workshop votes | Failed | Died in the second house |
| 2019 AB57 | Would have made some Clark and Washoe school board seats appointed instead of elected | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 SB111 | Would have made some Clark and Washoe school board seats appointed instead of elected | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2023 SB64 | Would have added appointed members to county school boards and required district audits | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2023 AB517 | Required recurring legislative performance audits of the two biggest districts and the charter authority | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- What have the first at-will principal removals under 82:SB292 looked like in practice?
- Does the new Public Education Oversight Board (83:SB460) publish anything close to the 'state of the school' participants asked for?
- Is superintendent compensation reachable by statute, or only through the elected boards that sign the contracts?

## 7. Increase teacher pay, support, and address the teacher shortage

- **Status:** Precedent exists; specific designs still stall
- **The record:** The raise happened: 82:SB231 (2023) sent state money to districts that budget salary increases for teachers and support staff (21-0, 41-1), and 83:AB398 (2025) funds extra pay for hard-to-fill positions (20-0, 41-1). The pipeline was rebuilt mostly unanimously: the Interstate Teacher Mobility Compact (82:SB442, 42-0), paid-pathway grants (82:AB515 and 82:AB428, both 42-0), student-teacher substitute rules (82:SB291), one-year provisional hiring of out-of-state teachers (83:AB49, 42-0 and 21-0), and recruiter-fee limits for visa teachers (83:AB472). What failed or died: the $45,000 minimum salary (80:SB445, R-sponsored, died in committee 2019), the 16-pupil grade-3 class-size cap with oversize pay (82:AB335), and the substitute-teacher insurance subsidy, which passed 31-11 and 16-4 and was vetoed (82:AB282). Bargaining expansions passed both houses and were vetoed twice (82:SB251; 83:AB155, the class-size-bargaining bill). Where similar ideas stopped: Salary floors and class-size caps die in the first committee; bargaining and benefit expansions die at the governor's desk. Everything about supply - licensure, compacts, pathways - passes nearly unanimously.
- **Where the decision sits:** State lever for money and licensure; actual salary schedules are district-union contracts. The 2023 state raise worked by conditioning new state money on districts budgeting raises - the tested template.

## 8. Measure student growth rather than fixed proficiency; broaden assessment beyond core testing

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** The purest 'measure the delta' bill died without a vote: 83:SB351 (2025) would have double-weighted the growth of the lowest-scoring pupils in school ratings; it died in Senate Education. The social-emotional measurement bill died on the Senate floor twice: 82:SB313 (2023) and 83:SB314 (2025), both aligning health standards with social-emotional learning standards and requiring teacher mental-health training, cleared committee and never got a floor vote. Alternative-assessment structures did somewhat better: competency-based education expansion passed the Senate 21-0 and died in the second house (83:SB403); the permanent competency-based approval process died in committee (83:AB24). What passed trims testing rather than redesigning it: 81:SB353 capped time spent on tests (42-0, 21-0), 82:SB9 ended end-of-course finals, and 83:AB401 - ending the college-and-career-readiness assessment as a diploma requirement - died in committee. Where similar ideas stopped: Senate Education for the growth-weighting bill; the Senate floor (twice) for social-emotional measurement; the second house for competency-based expansion in 2025.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state lever: the statewide accountability system and its star-rating formula are set by the State Board under statute, and Nevada's ratings already include a growth component - the failed bills changed its weight, not its existence.

## 9. Reform read-by-grade-three: reinstate retention, drop online assessment

- **Status:** Tried, but stalled
- **The record:** The record confirms the constituents' history and shows no bill going their direction since. Retention was removed by 80:AB289 (2019), which replaced automatic third-grade retention with mandatory intensive intervention (28-11, 17-4) - no bill since has proposed reinstating retention. No bill repeals the assessment requirement either; the closest was 83:AB386 (2025), letting Washoe County pilot an alternative literacy assessment: it passed the Assembly 40-0 and the Senate 21-0 as amended on the session's final days, then died awaiting an Assembly concurrence vote when time ran out. What did pass narrows the machinery: 83:SB278 (2025) dropped local assessments from deficiency decisions, leaving the statewide assessment as the trigger (42-0, 21-0). The phonics fight surfaced once: 82:AB187 (2023), banning three-cueing reading instruction in K-2, died in Assembly Education. Money: the 31st (2020) Special Session's AB3 struck the FY 2020-2021 $31.4 million Read by Grade 3 transfer during COVID cuts and made it a top restoration priority; 82:AB400 (2023) created the Early Childhood Literacy and Readiness Account. Where similar ideas stopped: The finish line for the alternative-assessment pilot (concurrence never happened); the first committee for the phonics ban and the 2021 literacy grant account (81:SB273, Senate Finance).
- **Where the decision sits:** A state lever: Read by Grade 3 is statute (NRS 392.750-392.760), and both the retention removal and the assessment rules were changed by bill. The specific assessment instrument is chosen by the State Board under that statute.

| Bill | What it tried | Result | Where it ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 AB289 | Ended automatic third-grade retention under Read by Grade 3, requiring intensive reading help instead | Enacted | Became law |
| 2025 AB386 | Would have let Washoe County pilot an alternative elementary literacy assessment | Failed | Passed both houses, still no law |
| 2025 SB278 | Improved licensure access and dropped local assessments from Read by Grade 3 deficiency decisions | Enacted | Became law |
| 2023 AB187 | Would have banned three-cueing reading instruction in kindergarten through grade 2 | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2021 SB273 | Would have funded a literacy grant account for elementary reading programs | Failed | Died in its first committee |
| 2023 AB400 | Education omnibus: early-literacy account, school-funding commission duties, charter transportation money | Enacted | Became law |

**Open questions for a working group:**
- Would the Washoe pilot (83:AB386) have answered the online-assessment complaint, and does its 40-0/21-0 record invite a refile?
- Ten years of results: what do the statewide reading numbers actually show since 2015 - the factual dispute participants could not settle?
- Is there any sponsor on record for reinstating retention, given the associations' standing objection?

## 10. Add school-based mental health services and integrated student/family supports

- **Status:** Precedent exists; specific designs still stall
- **The record:** The staffing framework is law: 80:SB319 (2019) defined school counselor, psychologist and social-worker roles and requires a full-time counselor where money allows (41-0, 21-0); 81:SB151 (2021) makes the two urban districts plan and report annually on improving support-staff ratios (33-8, 18-3); 83:SB277 (2025) does the same for social workers and eases their licensure pipeline (38-1, 15-5). Crisis infrastructure passed too: SafeVoice and Handle with Care (80:SB80), suicide-prevention policies (80:SB204), behavioral-health excused absences and crisis lines on pupil IDs (81:SB249), and a ban on AI doing counselors' mental-health work (83:AB406, 42-0). The mandates keep failing: making the full-time counselor unconditional (83:AB298) died in committee in 2025, monthly mental-health workshops (83:AB374) died in committee, and the well-being screening policy (83:SB254) died on the Senate floor. The governance layer hit the veto desk: the statewide children's mental health consortium (82:AB265) passed 42-0 and 20-0 and was vetoed in 2023, as was the behavioral-health oversight bill (82:AB201). Where similar ideas stopped: First committee for the unconditional mandates (they carry price tags); the governor's desk for children's mental-health governance in 2023.
- **Where the decision sits:** A state lever for frameworks and money; actual counselor hiring is district staffing constrained by the same shortage as teachers - which is why every enacted law says 'to the extent money is available' and the bill that removed those words died.

| 2025 SB277 | Required school social workers where money allows and eased their licensure pipeline | Enacted | Became law |
